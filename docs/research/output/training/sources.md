## The `strength-minimum-weekly` verdict

The round's most important output, recorded here and applied in `../recovery/`.

| Paper | What it is | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| Momma H et al. 2022, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061 | 16 cohorts | J-shaped; maximum risk reduction of 10–20% at about 30–60 min/week. **The card's figure verified** |
| Shailendra P et al. 2022, DOI 10.1016/j.amepre.2022.03.020 | 10 studies | The widely quoted 27% comes from a **four-study** dose-response arm. Thinner than it reads |
| Zhang Y et al. 2026, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2025-110503, PMID 42230125 | **147,374 people, 35,798 deaths, 30 years**, repeated biennial exposure | Abstract reports only the 90–119 band |

**The abstract does not give the curve. The full text does**, and it dissolves most of the apparent conflict:

| Minutes per week | Hazard ratio |
|---|---|
| 1–29 | 0.95 |
| 30–59 | **0.91** |
| 60–89 | 0.91 |
| 90–119 | 0.87 |
| ≥120 | **0.92** |

Almost all the benefit is bought by the first half hour. The 30–59 and 90–119 intervals overlap heavily. The top band being worse than the one below it marks the upper end as noise. And the cancer-mortality benefit appears **only** at 1–29 and 30–59 minutes, which argues *for* the card's number.

The two exposures also differ predictably: the 2026 cohort measured gym-style resistance training only (it says so, and notes it missed calisthenics and Pilates) and used repeated measures rather than a single baseline. Both push its apparent optimum upward.

**Verdict.** Keep 30 to 60 minutes across two sessions. Delete the claim that the association is strongest there, which is now contested and was never more than four cohorts. Add the two-hour ceiling. **Grade A to B** — not because the new paper contradicted the card, but because checking it showed the A rested on a concordance that no longer exists, everything is observational and self-reported, and a card in the same round sits at B on device-measured data.

Also new and uncarded: a 27% lower neurological-disease mortality association, grade C on one cohort family.

## Zone 2, verified fairly

A fourteen-expert panel could not agree a definition until 2025 and states the adaptations are probably **not unique** to that intensity. Storoschuk KL et al. 2025 finds the optimality claim unsupported — but it is a **narrative** review with an interested author, so it establishes that the claim is unsupported rather than that intervals win.

**The practice keeps B.** What comes off is the branding, the mitochondrial mechanism as popularly stated, and the watch zone as though it were measured. Best output: **when the week is short, protect the intensity**, which has three independent routes to it.

## The debunk that was declined

Discovery proposed retiring static stretching before lifting, citing the **chronic** stretching paper rather than the **acute** one.

The correct paper is Warneke K et al. 2024: 83 studies, 2,012 participants, effect size −0.21, meaningful loss **only at holds of 60 seconds or more**, and **no effect on jumping or sprinting**. Its authors explicitly reject excluding stretching from warm-ups.

So the honest statement is that long holds cost a little, short ones cost nothing, and the warm-up stays. Recorded as the round's clearest near-miss.
