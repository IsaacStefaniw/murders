# The Training coach's ladder

A pillar is not a list of good practices, it is an order. This one's order is governed by a question the coach should ask before anything else: **how much time is there actually?** Most training advice fails not because it is wrong but because it assumes a week the person does not have.

## Rung 0 — Is there a floor?

`strength-minimum-weekly` (recovery round, **B** after this round's regrade). Two sessions, thirty to sixty minutes in total, never a week at zero.

Everything else is optional. This is the rung with mortality data behind it, and the encouraging part is that almost all of the benefit arrives in the first half hour a week. There is no prize for piling on: past two hours the association turns back the other way.

## Rung 1 — When the week collapses

`one-set-week` (**C**). One hard set each of a squat, a press and a pull. Fifteen minutes.

Deliberately rung 1 rather than a fallback, because the bad week is the normal week for most people, and a ladder that only works in a good one is a ladder for somebody else. The researchers' own phrase, "suboptimal, yet significant", is the ethos of this rung.

## Rung 2 — Train the sets you are already doing, better

`add-one-to-your-estimate` (**C**) and `two-in-the-tank-for-strength` (**C**).

These cost no extra time at all. One corrects a measured one-repetition underestimate; the other says you do not need to reach failure if strength is the goal. Cheapest rung on the ladder, and nothing new goes in the diary.

## Rung 3 — Add what the goal actually needs

`load-the-tendons` (**C**) for anyone whose training is going well enough that the tissue is lagging behind the muscle. `ride-on-cardio-days` (**D**) for anyone doing both kinds of work.

## The parallel ladder, for people who do not train at all

Not a lower rung of the same ladder. A different one.

1. `exercise-snacks` (**B**) — three to five hard minutes scattered through an ordinary day. No gym, no change of clothes, and no decision to become someone who trains.
2. Then, if it takes, rung 0 above.

The gap between nothing and a little is the biggest gap in this pillar, and it does not require the person to reclassify themselves first.

## The over-sixty-five ladder

1. `steady-on-your-feet` (**B**) — balance and resistance together, two or three times a week, for at least three months. The strongest evidence in the pillar, and the trials found people preferred doing it alone at home and that adding components did not make it work better.
2. `move-one-lift-fast` (**C**) — power fades before strength does, and power is what catches you.

*Coach line:* "Balance and resistance, two or three times a week. Not a programme. The research found that adding more to it did not help."

## What the coach says when someone is stuck

**Stuck at rung 0 because there is no time.** Go to rung 1 rather than negotiating. Fifteen minutes exists in almost every week.

**Stuck on a plateau.** Rung 2 before rung 3. Most people are training slightly easier than they think, and adding volume before fixing that just adds fatigue.

**Asking about Zone 2.** The easy aerobic work is good and keeps its grade. The branding, the mitochondrial explanation and the watch zone are not measured things. And when the week is short, intensity is what to protect.

**Worried about stretching before lifting.** Long holds cost a little, short ones cost nothing, and the warm-up stays.

## What is deliberately not on the ladder

The existing `strength` card holds its A grade but needs a copy fix rather than a rung: its reasoning sells longevity while its summary says "most days", which nothing in this round supports.
