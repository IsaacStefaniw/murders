# Gate 1 — Podcast discovery: the CONNECTION pillar

Relationships · family · friendship · parenting · loneliness.
Run 8 September 2026 by the research session, against the shared
cross-field corpus index built at Gate 0 (836 episodes, 183 routed to
connection).

**What this file is.** Discovery only. Every claim below is written in
IntentNorth's own words; no transcript text is reproduced anywhere in it,
not a sentence. Nothing here is graded, and nothing here is evidence. It
names what to verify, in what order, and whose name can honestly go on a
card. Gate 2 decides what is true; Gate 4 writes the protocols.

**The pillar's standing constraint, restated so it is not lost.**
`BRIEF-relationships.md` forbids an A grade in this pillar and that is
deliberate. This round found nothing that changes that, and section D
explains why the strongest thing in the pillar — social connection and
mortality — is still observational and still not an A.

**A second constraint specific to this pillar.** The brief says to treat
this genre's podcasts with more suspicion than any other, because it is
dominated by practitioners selling courses. That warning is borne out
below and quantified in section G: **155 of the 183 connection-routed
episodes — 85 per cent — come from five shows** whose relationship output
is consistently practitioner-led and course-adjacent, and only about 170
of the 183 are distinct conversations once clip feeds and re-titled
repeats are collapsed. The mining below is
weighted deliberately away from them and toward the handful of episodes
where a researcher is actually in the room.

### A6. Three guests the index named wrongly, and why it matters

The Gate 0 index routes on episode titles, and three of the most useful
episodes in the round are titled in a way that hides who is actually in
them.

| what the title suggests | who is actually in it | consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "Harvard psychologist shares 6 words that will change your family" — reads as Robert Waldinger and the Harvard Study of Adult Development | **Stuart Ablon**, Massachusetts General / Think:Kids, co-developer with Ross Greene of Collaborative Problem Solving | **The single most important find of the round for the family block**, reachable only by opening it. A search for Ross Greene by name returns zero |
| "The reality of adult friendship" — reads as Marisa Franco, the obvious guest for that title | **Kasley Killam**, social-health researcher and author | The friendship block's main source, found by accident |
| "It's not you, why your family stresses you out" and "You'll never see your family the same after this episode" — read as two episodes | **One conversation with Marielle Bouquet**, published twice under different titles | A convergence trap: counted naively it would look like two independent voices |

**The method note.** Name search alone would have missed all three, and
would also have missed John Gottman entirely (A7). Topic search alone
would have missed the roster guests on guest-titled shows. Both are
needed, and the episode has to be opened before the guest can be trusted.

## C. CONVERGENCE

**Convergence sets verification order. It never sets a grade and it is
never evidence.** In this pillar it also has to be scored more
suspiciously than anywhere else, because a large share of the apparent
agreement is the same one lab's numbers arriving through eight different
mouths.

Two counting rules were applied. **Two episodes by the same person are
one voice** — so Duhigg's two shows count once, and the Mel Robbins /
Diary of a CEO duplicate slugs count once. And **a voice that names its
source on air is not independent of that source** — Jay Shetty citing
Gottman is Gottman, not a second reading.

### C4. The Gottman problem, handled properly

The brief asked for both sides. Both sides exist and both are findable.

**What the podcasts say.** The Diary of a CEO episode is promoted on the
claim that fifty years of research lets the Gottmans predict divorce with
ninety per cent accuracy. On Jay Shetty the host puts it at over ninety
per cent and John Gottman answers the question without disputing the
figure, going on to describe the physiological and behavioural predictors.
This is the most-repeated quantitative claim in the entire pillar.

**What is genuinely solid underneath it.** A large body of *observational*
work: couples were recorded, their interactions coded, their physiology
measured, and they were followed for years. The four negative behaviours,
the positive-to-negative ratio, the response to bids, and the finding that
most conflicts are never resolved all come out of that, and the
descriptive part of it is real and useful. The bids figures — roughly a
third versus roughly six-in-seven — are the most concrete numbers in the
round.

**What the criticism actually says.** Heyman & Smith Slep, *The Hazards of
Predicting Divorce Without Crossvalidation*, Journal of Marriage and
Family 2001, 63(2), 473–479 — doi 10.1111/j.1741-3737.2001.00473.x,
PMID 17066126, free full text at PMC1622921, Crossref shows no retraction
or update. Their argument, in our words: prediction models built and
tested on the same sample will look extremely accurate; when the equations
are applied to fresh data, and when the actual base rate of divorce in the
population is taken into account, both the accuracy and the predictive
value fall away sharply. They name Gottman, Coan, Carrère & Swanson 1998
directly and conclude that any prediction study without cross-validation
should be read with extreme caution however impressive it looks.

**The third thing, which is the interesting one.** On Jay Shetty, John
Gottman says of his own hypotheses over the decades that he turns out to
be wrong about sixty per cent of the time, and that without the data he
would assume he was right every time. That is a more honest epistemic
statement than anything else recorded in this round, and it sits about
four minutes away from an unchallenged ninety-per-cent claim in the same
conversation. **The overclaiming in this pillar is mostly done by hosts
and episode marketing, not by the researcher.** That distinction should
survive into how the library credits him.

**What Gate 2 should do.** Grade the observational findings on their own
merits — they may well support a C, possibly a B for the bids work given
the size and the follow-up length. Do not put a prediction-accuracy
percentage on any card at any grade. And where a card carries a Gottman
number, the `sources.md` row should carry Heyman & Smith Slep beside it,
because a reader who knows the field knows that criticism exists and the
card is more trustworthy for having met it.

### D1. The finding that inverts the whole friendship block

**Masi, Chen, Hawkley & Cacioppo, *A meta-analysis of interventions to
reduce loneliness*, Personality and Social Psychology Review 2011,
15(3), 219–266.** doi 10.1177/1088868310377394 · PMID 20716644 ·
PMC3865701 · 1,035 citations · Crossref clean.

Four families of loneliness intervention have been tried: teaching social
skills, adding social support, creating more opportunities for contact,
and working on the way a lonely person reads social situations. Pooling
them, **the single-group and non-randomised studies produced the biggest
effects — and among the randomised comparisons, the interventions that
worked best were the ones that addressed how the person interprets social
situations, not the ones that supplied more contact.**

**Why this matters more than anything else in the round.** Every
communicator in the corpus prescribes more contact. Killam's 5-3-1, Mel
Robbins's get-off-the-couch, Ferriss's shared activities, Shetty's
routines — all four are the third strategy, which is the one the
randomised evidence supports least. Nobody in 183 connection-routed
episodes mentions the social-cognition finding.

**What it would card as.** Something closer to: before the next social
thing, write down what you expect the other people to think of you, and
afterwards write what actually happened. Or the version already
well-evidenced elsewhere in the corpus — the systematic overestimate of
how badly a reach-out will land, which the live `friend-unprompted-message`
card already carries via Epley and Liu.

**Attribution: empty, or Cacioppo and Hawkley as researchers**, subject to
the author-list rule. Note that John Cacioppo is already credited on
`closeness-audit` and is on this paper's author list, so a researcher
credit there would pass.

**Does Peterson teach it?** Not this practice. He talks about children

**Recommendation.** Replace with **Sheila Eyberg · Carolyn

cleanest of the six: the right names were already in the library, on the

### F3. `carer-ask-for-cover` — "Ask for cover, once a week"

*Ten minutes on Sunday: name one thing someone else could carry this week, and ask them.*

**Does Peterson teach it?** No. Nothing on carer burden anywhere

**Does anyone else?** **No.** This is the emptiest patch in the whole

Meanwhile the literature is substantial: four meta-analyses between 222

for practical help rather than waiting until it is bad enough — sits

**Recommendation. An empty attribution is the honest answer.** There is

This is the card where the flag was most worth raising: it is about carers,
it is about asking for help, and it currently carries a politically
polarising name for no traceable reason at all.

### G1. The pillar-level risk, measured rather than asserted

The brief says to treat this genre's podcasts with more suspicion than any

### G2. Jordan Peterson — the roster's named risk, and this is his pillar

### G4. Do not attribute, and consider naming as declined — Erica Komisar

Two index rows, one conversation, 25,000+ words, and the argument is that
mothers specifically — not a consistent responsive caregiver — are
developmentally necessary, that daycare is dangerous, and that rising
childhood mental-illness diagnosis tracks maternal absence.

The evidence offered is: three literatures named as bodies of work with no
individual studies cited, the pattern in her own clinical caseload, and
one unnamed UK study about parents in bed with a crying baby.

**Three reasons this is flagged harder than the others.** The attachment
literature she invokes is about caregiver consistency and responsiveness,
not about mothers as a category, so the citation does not support the
claim. Clinical-caseload inference is presented as evidence. And the
practical implication — that a working mother is harming her child — is
aimed at people who mostly cannot act on it, in a pillar whose brief says
nothing should imply somebody is failing at their family.

**Nothing ships from this episode.** It is a reasonable candidate for the
brief's "explicit list of famous findings you checked and declined to use".

## H. TIME-BACK CANDIDATES

Roughly one in five of what the round returned, as the contract asks, and
framed the way the money and work rounds framed it: **what somebody can
stop doing, and the finding that lets them stop.** These are Gate 1
candidates — the pointer is here, the verification is Gate 2's.

In this pillar, time back matters more than usual. The people who reach
for a relationship coach are already trying hard, often at a bad moment.
Telling someone they can stop doing something is a warmer act here than
telling them to start.

| # | stop doing | what lets you stop | who it frees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Counting your positives to a ratio.** Five to one, three to one, twenty to one — stop scoring the marriage | The 5:1 ratio comes from one observational lab and describes what happy couples were observed doing, not a target that produces happiness. The related Fredrickson–Losada positivity ratio has already been formally corrected and the Work round documented it. Nothing here supports a scoreboard | Anyone who has been told to hit a number, which by now is most people who have read anything about relationships |
| 2 | **Taking a relationship compatibility test, or typing yourself and your partner** | Explicitly, on air, credited to Eli Finkel's research: **nothing measurable in two people as individuals predicts whether they will like each other.** The signal is only in the interaction | People deciding whether to keep dating someone based on a quiz |
| 3 | **Working out your attachment style and treating it as an explanation** | Five apparent voices, one lineage, one popular book. The adult-attachment research is real; the four-box self-typing that the podcasts teach is not what it supports, and the brief flagged this before the round started | Anyone currently reading their own behaviour through a label |
| 4 | **Learning your partner's love language and speaking it** | Direct tests of Chapman's predictions are mixed to negative. Not refuted, not supported, and certainly not the master key it is sold as | Couples doing an assessment instead of a conversation |
| 5 | **Believing you can predict whether your relationship will end** | Heyman & Smith Slep 2001: divorce-prediction accuracy collapses once the equations are cross-validated and the base rate is taken into account. The ninety-per-cent figure is mostly repeated by hosts and episode marketing, not by the researcher | Anyone who watched a clip and is now auditing their own conversations for the four horsemen |
| 6 | **Trying to resolve the recurring argument** | About sixty-nine per cent of a couple's conflicts are never resolved and are managed instead. The recurring one is not evidence of failure; it is the normal case | Couples on their ninth attempt at settling the same thing |
| 7 | **Scheduling date night as a divorce-prevention measure** | One observational study, from a source with an interest in the answer. The brief flagged date night specifically and the round confirms why | People forcing a Friday that neither of them wants |
| 8 | **Prescribing yourself more social contact when you feel lonely** | **The most useful thing in the round.** Masi et al. 2011: among randomised comparisons, the loneliness interventions that worked best addressed how the person reads social situations — not the ones that supplied more contact. Every voice in the corpus prescribes more contact; nobody mentions this | The person who has been told to join a club and feels worse for having tried |
| 9 | **Hitting five different people a week, three close friends and an hour a day** | The 5-3-1 rule is a book's mnemonic, not a measured threshold. There is no dose-response finding behind those three numbers | Anyone currently failing an invented quota |
| 10 | **Counting the hours to a friendship** | Jeffrey Hall's hours finding is real in shape, and the numbers as repeated on air do not match the published ones (#57–58). The shape — adult friendship takes far more shared time than student friendship — is the usable part; the arithmetic is not | People treating a friendship as a progress bar |
| 11 | **Face-to-face sit-downs with a teenager who does not want one** | Traceable to Tim Ferriss as a practice and flagged by him as evolutionary reasoning rather than evidence — but the negative half stands on its own: nothing in the corpus or the literature supports the interview format, and the live card already says the time has to be theirs to fill | Every parent who has scheduled a talk and had it fail |
| 12 | **Trying to change a family member's mind** | Bill Doherty's prime directive, and Duhigg's supporting point that better facts do not move someone who has spent hours acquiring a position | Anyone dreading a family gathering for this reason |
| 13 | **Wrapping the concern up first.** Leading a hard conversation with your own perspective, however gently phrased | Ablon's process analysis of recorded sessions: it is the first ingredient going wrong that wrecks Plan B, and it goes wrong by starting with your own view. Their concern comes first, entirely | Parents who have been taught to lead with an "I feel" statement |
| 14 | **Solving it.** Answering an emotional problem with a practical solution | Duhigg's matching principle, in two independent episodes, plus the three-word version: heard, helped or hugged. Both people are being reasonable and both feel unheard | The partner who has been getting this wrong for years and did not know there was a question to ask |
| 15 | **Guilt about the mother/daycare question** | Section G4. The claim is stronger than the literatures cited support, the literature is about caregiver consistency rather than about mothers, and the round declines to build anything on it | A large share of this app's likely audience |
| 16 | **Cancelling because you are tired — with one carve-out that is not negotiable** | The tiredness that makes you cancel on a friend is usually the thing the friend would have fixed. Killam's own carve-out is the good part: if it is genuine medical exhaustion, resting is the need and this does not apply | People who need permission to go, and separately, people who need permission to stay home |

**One thing that is deliberately not on this list.** Nothing here tells
anyone to stop a small daily kindness. The strongest genuine convergence
in the round (C1) is that the small daily response is what carries a
relationship, and the time back in this pillar comes from dropping the
measurement, the typologies and the predictions — not from dropping the
practice.

## Closing notes for the gates downstream

**Gate 2 — the four things to verify first.**
1. The Gottman bids figures and the 5:1 ratio, read against Heyman & Smith
   Slep 2001. Both sides in the sources row.
2. Masi et al. 2011 in full, because the social-cognition finding inverts
   the whole friendship block and nobody in the corpus has noticed.
3. Whether a child's outcome tracks interparental conflict rather than
   marital status (Amato; Cummings & Davies), which is the round's best
   new family finding and rests on one podcast voice.
4. The randomised joint-account work Wilcox pointed at — an actual
   experiment in a pillar with almost none, and it cross-references the
   money brief.

**Also for Gate 2:** pull the Huberman Lab Perel episode's references list
from hubermanlab.com, since the aggregator route is a stub (A1); find the
ninety-one-study gratitude meta-analysis Jay Shetty cites (#105); and use
OpenAlex or Crossref rather than `harvest.js lit` for division of labour,
the mental load, joint accounts and the love-languages tests, because
Europe PMC indexes those literatures thinly (D12).

**Gate 3.** Two practices found here are cross-pillar and should get one
card with multiple `goalDomains` rather than duplicates: the pause before
responding (connection and mind) and the joint-versus-separate accounts
question (connection and wealth).

**Gate 5.** Jay Shetty's daily / weekly / monthly / seasonal split (#106)
is the closest thing the round found to a ladder for this pillar, and it
is worth taking as a starting shape even though it is Tier 2 material: the
one-minute daily thing comes before the weekly conversation, which comes
before the booked trip. The friendship ladder is different and Masi
suggests it starts with how the person expects to be received, not with
how many people they see.

**The integrity test holds.** Nothing in this round is an A. The closest is
the social-connection-and-mortality work, and it is observational, it is
about association rather than about a practice, and the brief's judgement
that it earns a careful B or C with the reasoning written out is the right
one. No A is proposed.

**And the tone.** The best-evidenced things in this pillar are small,
cheap and unglamorous: answer the thing they said, say the specific thing
you noticed, ask what they want before you answer, put the call in the
diary. The honest position is not that none of this is proven — it is that
these are decent, humane things people who care about each other do, the
evidence is modest, and doing them on purpose is better than not.
