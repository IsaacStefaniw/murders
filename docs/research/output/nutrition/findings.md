# Nutrition round — findings

Seven candidates, one regrade downward, three time-back items. Gate 1 mined nutrition and training together; Gate 2 opened every load-bearing paper's full text rather than its abstract, which is what produced the round's central finding.

## 1. The protein number should not be written, and this is the clearest case the library has

Four of the roster's Tier 1 voices state **1.6 grams per kilogram per day** as settled. Every route leads back to one paper. Reading that paper's full text:

| What is quoted | What the paper says |
|---|---|
| A break point at 1.6 | **1.62 at p = 0.079.** Not significant, and the paper's own figure caption says so |
| A confident threshold | **95% confidence interval 1.03 to 2.20**, on an analysed range of 0.9 to 2.4. Roughly four-fifths of the data |
| 49 studies, 1,863 people | The break-point analysis rests on **42 study arms and 723 people** |
| The paper recommends 1.6 | **Its discussion recommends about 2.2.** The quoted number comes from the abstract's closing sentence |

Two further details point the wrong way for a ceiling. Higher baseline intake was associated with a *larger* benefit, which is not the shape a threshold makes. And later, larger work finds the curve bending rather than stopping: one review of 105 studies and 5,402 people finds the slope still positive well beyond, and another by the same senior author finds no break point at all.

There is also a size question nobody quotes. Across those trials, protein added about **9% to the strength gain and 27% to the lean-mass gain that the training produced on its own**. Protein is the smaller half of that partnership.

**So the direction survives and the number does not.** Most people eat less protein than would help them, more helps up to a point nobody has located, and the honest card names a range rather than a point. The library's existing `meal-sketch` already does this and is already correct.

**Stated fairly**, because the roster asks for that and it matters here. These are four of the most rigorous communicators in the category and they are quoting a real meta-analysis accurately from its abstract. The failure is that the abstract's closing sentence says something the paper's own analysis does not support. That is the paper's failure before it is theirs, and this is enthusiasm rather than dishonesty. It is also exactly why a library that reads full texts is worth building.

## 2. A claim I reported was withdrawn at this gate

Discovery reported that people find time-restricted eating easier to start later than to stop earlier, and that doing it inconsistently leaves them hungrier than not doing it at all. I passed that on.

**It does not hold.** All three of those findings trace to a **conference-proceedings abstract**, authored by the company's own co-founder and chief executive, with no control group, self-reported data, and no peer-reviewed publication anywhere. Europe PMC was searched by title and by every author. It is not citeable and no card here rests on it.

That is the second time this session that discovery has carried a plausible number one gate further than it should have gone. Both were caught before shipping. The structure works, and the lesson is that a claim is not verified because it was interesting.

## 3. The meal-timing mechanism is wrong, and the finding survives

The physiology verified well. Identical meals eaten later produce substantially worse glucose responses, across several small controlled studies.

**But the popular explanation is wrong.** The trial usually invoked for "you burn more when you eat earlier" found no difference in energy expenditure, resting metabolic rate, or weight lost. What differed was hunger. So the reason to eat earlier is appetite and overnight glucose, not calorie burning, and any copy claiming otherwise should be corrected.

`protein-breakfast` moves **B to C**. The per-meal protein ceiling and the leucine threshold, which are the ideas underneath it, do not survive: the systematic review finds no threshold in either age group.

## 5. Time back

- **Hydration and thinking.** Both meta-analyses find no significant cognitive effect below about 2% body-mass loss, which is a great deal more dehydration than being a bit behind on water. Minutes returned: the low-grade anxiety about the bottle.
- **The per-meal protein ceiling.** No threshold found in either age group. You do not need to distribute it as carefully as you have been told.
- **The satiety claim about fat and fibre** turns out to rest on a null result with twelve studies in it.
- **The "brain rewiring" study.** Confirmed overstated: 29 men, five days, unequal allocation, never replicated. One source describes a good trial accurately and then describes this one as brain rewiring, in the same place.

## 6. What was routed elsewhere

Every supplement claim went to the carve-out under its own rules rather than being written as nutrition. Creatine should split into a muscle entry and a separate, much weaker brain entry, on the precedent the carve-out already sets. Collagen was declined: no position stand names an amount, and the mechanism study that is usually cited comes from a laboratory that has since published a null.

## 8. What the Nutrition coach can now say on a Tuesday

- "Same shopping list, one step less processed. In the trial where everything else was matched, people ate five hundred more calories a day on the processed version without choosing to."
- "On nights, eat properly before and after and keep the middle of the shift light. Never skip it."
- "Bad night? Decide lunch now, while you can. Tiredness genuinely changes what you want, and that is physiology rather than discipline."
- "You do not need to hit a protein number at every meal. The threshold that idea rests on has not been found."
- "Drink when you are thirsty. Being slightly behind on water does not measurably affect your thinking."
- "For cholesterol it is four things together, not one. Individually they do almost nothing."

## 9. Product notes

- **No card in this round names a protein amount**, deliberately, and the safety line on the protein card says why in one clause. That is unusual and it is the point.
