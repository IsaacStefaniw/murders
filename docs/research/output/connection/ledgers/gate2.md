## 0. HEADLINE — what changed at this gate

**1. The Masi finding survives, but Gate 1 overstated it, and the
corrected version is better.** Gate 1 said the loneliness result
"inverts the friendship block". It does not invert it; it *splits* it,
and the split is cleaner and more useful than the inversion would have
been. Zagic et al. 2022 — 58 randomised trials — separates the two
outcomes that everybody else merges: **giving people access to other
people is the best way to raise how much contact they actually have
(g = 0.67). Giving people skills for the way they read social situations
is the best way to reduce how lonely they feel (g = −0.53).** Both work.
They work on different things. Every communicator in the corpus
prescribes the first and calls it a cure for the second.

That is the round's headline, and it is a *sharper* claim than Gate 1's,
because it does not require telling anybody their club membership was
wasted.

**2. The 90 per cent divorce prediction does not survive, and neither
does a weaker version of it.** Heyman & Smith Slep 2001 is real, is
free, is unretracted, and is worse for the prediction claim than Gate 1
described. But the observational half — the four behaviours, the bids,
the perpetual problems — is a different literature with a different
problem: it is real, it is replicated in shape, and it has almost never
been tested as an instruction. It stays at C and D, which is where the
live library already has it.

**3. The 15-cigarettes line is not in the papers.** Holt-Lunstad 2010
reports an odds ratio, and the comparison in its discussion is not the
equivalence the podcast genre repeats. Section A3 gives the actual
numbers.

**4. The literature inlet produced more shippable material than the
podcast inlet did**, again — capitalisation, perceived partner
responsiveness, interparental conflict, and the Cochrane parenting
reviews between them carry more weight than all 183 connection-routed
episodes.

**5. Two Gate 1 candidates did not survive contact with the papers**:
the "91 gratitude studies" specificity claim (section G) and the
randomised joint-account work as Wilcox described it (section A9).

### A1. Loneliness — work on how you expect to be received, not only on how often you go out

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Masi CM, Chen HY, Hawkley LC, Cacioppo JT. *A meta-analysis of interventions to reduce loneliness.* Personality and Social Psychology Review 2011;15(3):219–266 | 10.1177/1088868310377394 · PMID 20716644 · PMC3865701 | Crossref `retraction_or_update: none`; no `DO_NOT_CITE`; OpenAlex not retracted. 1,035 citations |
| 2 | Zagic D, Wuthrich VM, Rapee RM, Wolters N. *Interventions to improve social connections: a systematic review and meta-analysis.* Soc Psychiatry Psychiatr Epidemiol 2022;57:885–906 | 10.1007/s00127-021-02191-w · PMID 34796368 | Clean. 44 citations |
| 3 | Hickin N, Käll A, Shafran R, Sutcliffe S, Manzotti G, Langan D. *The effectiveness of psychological interventions for loneliness.* Clinical Psychology Review 2021;88:102066 | 10.1016/j.cpr.2021.102066 · PMID 34339939 | Crossref `none`. 158 citations |
| 4 | Lasgaard M, Qualter P, Løvschall C, Laustsen LM, Lim MH, et al. *Are loneliness interventions effective for reducing loneliness? A meta-analytic review of 280 studies.* American Psychologist 2026;81:36–52 | 10.1037/amp0001578 · PMID 41129341 | Crossref `none`. 7 citations (new) |

**Design and n**

- **Masi 2011**: meta-analysis, 50 studies — 12 single-group pre-post, 18
  non-randomised comparisons, **20 randomised comparisons**. Four
  intervention families coded: social skills, social support, increased
  opportunity for contact, and maladaptive social cognition.
- **Zagic 2022**: systematic review and meta-analysis of **58 randomised
  controlled trials**, mean participant age 62.
- **Hickin 2021**: systematic review of 31 RCTs (N = 3,959); meta-analysis
  of 28 (N = 3,039). Screened 3,973 abstracts and 103 full texts.
  Cochrane Risk of Bias tool applied.
- **Lasgaard 2026**: preregistered systematic review, 312 studies
  identified, **280 meta-analysed** — 122 RCTs, 33 multicohort, 118
  single-arm. GRADE applied.

**Key results, with numbers, and which source confirmed which**

- Masi 2011, read from the PMC full text (Table 4): design predicts effect
  size. Single-group pre-post **−0.367 (95% CI −0.55 to −0.18)**;
  non-randomised comparison **−0.459 (−0.72 to −0.20)**; **randomised
  comparison −0.198 (−0.32 to −0.08)**. So the weaker the design the
  bigger the effect, by a factor of more than two. *Confirmed from the
  PMC full text.*
- Masi 2011, the moderator: among the randomised comparisons, the
  strategy that did best was the one addressing maladaptive social
  cognition. The commonly quoted figure for that subgroup is **−0.598**.
  *The direction is confirmed from the paper's own abstract, which I read
  at Europe PMC. The −0.598 figure I could only confirm from secondary
  reporting — the PMC table route returned a CAPTCHA and the mirrored PDF
  had moved. Marked **PARTLY VERIFIED**, and no card should print it.*
- **Zagic 2022 is the row that matters most**, and it is the independent
  replication Gate 1 asked for. Across 58 RCTs, interventions improved
  **objective social contact g = 0.43** and **perceived quality of social
  connection g = −0.33**. Split by strategy: **increasing access to other
  people was the best strategy for objective contact (g = 0.67)**, and
  **teaching people to handle maladaptive attributions, fear-driven
  avoidance of social situations and the practical barriers to contact was
  the best strategy for perceived quality (g = −0.53)**. *All four numbers
  read directly from the Europe PMC abstract record.*
- Hickin 2021: psychological interventions reduce loneliness against
  control, **g = 0.43**, small-to-medium, across 28 RCTs. Intervention
  type as a moderator was *approaching* significance, not significant.
  Considerable heterogeneity. CBT was the most common intervention.
- Lasgaard 2026, the largest and newest: short-term effect on loneliness
  **SMD −0.50 (95% CI −0.60 to −0.39) across 122 RCTs**; −0.51 across 33
  multicohort; −0.38 across 118 single-arm. Long-term effects at one to
  six months comparable to short-term. **Psychological interventions were
  the most effective strategy (moderate effect); social and emotional
  skills training, social-network interventions and social-support
  interventions were small to moderate.** No significant difference by age
  group. **GRADE confidence: low or very low.**

**Limitations and replication**

The direction replicates four times across fifteen years and independent
teams: **working on the way a person reads social situations beats
supplying more contact, when the outcome is felt loneliness.** That is
about as good as replication gets in this pillar.

Four honest limits.

1. **Contact-based approaches are not useless, and Gate 1's "inverts the
   friendship block" is too strong.** Zagic shows they are the *best*
   strategy for the objective outcome, and Lasgaard 2026 finds
   social-network and social-support interventions small to moderate
   rather than null. The correct statement is that they raise contact more
   than they lower loneliness.
2. **Lasgaard's GRADE rating is low to very low.** The newest, largest and
   best-conducted review in this space says its own confidence in the
   estimates is poor. That alone caps the grade.
3. **Every one of these is a meta-analysis of clinician-delivered
   multi-session interventions.** Not one tested a self-administered
   two-minute expectation-check of the kind a card would ask for. The gap
   between "CBT for loneliness across eight weeks" and "write down what
   you expect people to think of you before you go" is the whole
   inferential distance, and it is not small.
4. Masi's populations skew older and institutional; Zagic's mean age is
   62. Generalising to a 35-year-old who has moved city is an extension.

**Grade of the practice: C.**

*Why C and not B.* B in this library means tested and it held up. What
held up is a *class* of professionally delivered psychological
intervention, not the practice a card can ask for. The specific card —
notice and write down what you expect the other people to think, then
check it afterwards — has never been trialled as a standalone. It has
strong mechanism support, four converging meta-analyses behind its
direction, and a well-evidenced sibling in the corpus already (the
reach-out underestimation work behind `friend-unprompted-message`, which
is a B). C is the honest level for an untested distillation of a
well-supported class.

*Why C and not D.* Because the direction is not a hunch. Four independent
meta-analyses, one of them preregistered with 280 studies, agree on which
family of intervention does best on the felt outcome. D would understate
that.

#### A2a. The prediction claim — UNSUPPORTED, and the number is real but means something else

**What the papers actually say.** Gottman & Levenson 2000's own abstract,
read at OpenAlex, states that the model predicted divorce with **93 per
cent accuracy** over the 14-year period. So the podcast figure is not
invented by hosts — it is in the papers. Gate 1 was right that hosts do
the loudest overclaiming, and slightly wrong to imply the number has no
scholarly source. It has one. The source is the problem.

**What Heyman & Smith Slep 2001 did to it**, read from the free PMC full
text:

- Design: reanalysis of archival data from the **1985 National Family
  Violence Survey**. 176 divorced participants, plus 176 married or
  cohabiting with the highest disagreement scores and 176 with the
  lowest — **n = 528 retained**, randomly split into a development
  subsample (n = 264) and a crossvalidation subsample (n = 264).
- **Development sample: 89.71 per cent overall accuracy** (sensitivity
  91.89, specificity 89.22). This is the kind of number the genre quotes.
- **Crossvalidation sample: 69.31 per cent** overall accuracy, and
  **sensitivity collapses from 91.89 to 45.95**. Out of sample the model
  misses more than half the divorces it is meant to catch.
- Apply the realistic base rate — they use **16 per cent** divorce
  prevalence for couples married three to six years — and the **positive
  predictive value falls to 20.99 per cent.** A couple flagged as heading
  for divorce is right about one time in five.
- On Gottman, Coan, Carrère & Swanson 1998 specifically: the prediction
  analyses rested on **n = 60 couples**, with a weighted multi-variable
  equation. That is textbook overfitting territory.
- Their conclusion, in our words: initial predictive results can help
  identify risk factors, but publicising "predictive power" in the
  popular media has to wait until sensitivity, specificity and predictive
  value have been shown on an independent sample.

**Verdict: VERIFIED, and decisive.** The 90-plus per cent figure is a
model-fitting statistic from small samples with no crossvalidation. A
comparable model loses twenty points of accuracy and two-thirds of its
sensitivity out of sample, and a positive prediction is wrong four times
in five once the base rate is applied.

**Grade of the practice — "watch for these signs and predict your
relationship's future": E, and it does not ship at any grade.** Not
"unproven pending more work": contradicted by the only crossvalidation
anyone has run. **No card carries a prediction-accuracy figure. No card
frames the four behaviours as a divorce test.** This is a time-back
entry, not a practice.

#### A2b. The observational findings — what survives

The descriptive work is a different literature and it survives on
different terms.

- **Driver & Gottman 2004** is the peer-reviewed paper closest to the
  "bids" material. Design: **observational, 49 newlywed couples**, a
  10-minute dinnertime interaction and a 15-minute conflict discussion,
  SPAFF-coded, analysed by path analysis. Result: everyday playfulness
  and enthusiasm at dinner ran ahead of humour and affection during
  conflict, and the path model fitted better in that direction than the
  reverse. **The authors state in their own abstract that the work is
  strictly observational and correlational and that the direction of
  causation is undetermined.** Gate 1's claim #24 — that turning toward
  produced humour spontaneously — is this paper, and this paper says it
  cannot establish that.
- **The 33 per cent versus 86 per cent bids figures could not be traced
  to any peer-reviewed paper.** Searched at Europe PMC and by open web
  search: every route leads to the Gottman Institute's own materials and
  the 2001 trade book *The Relationship Cure*. Gate 1 called these "the
  most concrete numbers in the round"; at this gate they are
  **UNVERIFIED**, and under `RETRACTIONS.md`'s "numbers that trace to
  nothing" rule they must not be printed on a card or in a `why`.
- **The 5:1 ratio.** Traceable to Gottman's 1993 balance-theory paper and
  the 1994 trade book, not to any independent replication I could find.
  Worth noting that the 1993 paper's own framing is catastrophe theory
  applied to marital interaction — the same mathematical family as the
  Fredrickson–Losada positivity ratio that `RETRACTIONS.md` already
  records as formally withdrawn. **That is an adjacency, not an
  equivalence, and this gate is not claiming the Gottman ratio is
  withdrawn.** What is recorded is that the ratio is one lab's
  descriptive statistic with no independent replication located.
- **The 69 per cent perpetual-problems figure**: same status — Institute
  and trade-book provenance, no paper found. Do not print the number. The
  *shape* — most recurring disagreements get managed rather than solved —
  is safe to say without it.

#### A2c. The independent finding that caps the whole block

**Lavner, Karney & Bradbury 2016** is the counterweight Gate 1 did not
have, and after Heyman it is the most important paper in this section.

Design: newlywed couples observed **four times at nine-month intervals**
on positivity, negativity and effectiveness, with matched self-reports of
relationship satisfaction; cross-lagged longitudinal analysis.

Result: cross-sectionally, more satisfied couples do communicate more
positively and less negatively — the association everyone quotes is
there. Longitudinally, **both** communication-to-satisfaction and
satisfaction-to-communication paths were detectable, and **neither was
robust**. The authors say in their own abstract that this raises
important doubts about theories and interventions that treat couple
communication skills as *the* key predictor of satisfaction.

**This is the honest ceiling for every communication-skills card in this
pillar.** It does not say the practices are worthless. It says the causal
story they are sold with — fix the communication and the relationship
follows — is not supported, and the arrow may run partly the other way.

It stays in the library because it is free, kind, harmless and converged on by four independent traditions, which is a reason to *offer* it and never a reason to grade it up |

*Why the bids practice is not C.* C means some evidence, not settled.
Here it is one lab, one design, n = 49 at the peer-reviewed end, headline
numbers unlocatable, and an independent longitudinal study finding the
causal path weak in both directions. D is already generous. The live
library has `turning-toward` at D, which was the right call and stands.

### A3. Social connection and mortality — the strongest evidence in the pillar, and still not an A

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | Holt-Lunstad J, Smith TB, Layton JB. *Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review.* PLoS Medicine 2010;7:e1000316 | 10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 · PMID 20668659 · PMC2910600 | Crossref `none`. **Open access.** 3,809 citations |
| 13 | Holt-Lunstad J, Smith TB, Baker M, Harris T, Stephenson D. *Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality.* Perspectives on Psychological Science 2015;10:227–237 | 10.1177/1745691614568352 · PMID 25910392 | Crossref `none`. 3,239 citations |
| 14 | Wang F, Gao Y, Han Z, et al. *A systematic review and meta-analysis of 90 cohort studies of social isolation, loneliness and mortality.* Nature Human Behaviour 2023;7:1307–1319 | 10.1038/s41562-023-01617-6 · PMID 37337095 | Crossref `none`. 281 citations. **PROSPERO CRD42022299959** |
| 15 | Steptoe A, Shankar A, Demakakos P, Wardle J. *Social isolation, loneliness, and all-cause mortality in older men and women.* PNAS 2013;110:5797–5801 | 10.1073/pnas.1219686110 · PMID 23530191 · PMC3625264 | Crossref `none`. 1,528 citations |

**Design, n and results — every number read from the source named**

- **Holt-Lunstad 2010** (Europe PMC abstract, plus the open-access PLoS
  article for the discussion): meta-analysis of **148 studies, 308,849
  participants**, mean age at baseline **63.9 years**, mean follow-up
  **7.5 years**. Random-effects **OR = 1.50 (95% CI 1.42–1.59)** — a 50
  per cent higher likelihood of survival for people with stronger social
  relationships. Consistent across age, sex, baseline health, cause of
  death and follow-up length. **Measurement matters a lot**:
  multidimensional assessments of social integration gave the strongest
  association (about a **91 per cent** increase in odds of survival),
  while a binary "do you live alone" was the *least* predictive variable
  in the set.
- **Holt-Lunstad 2015**: meta-analysis, studies from January 1980 to
  February 2014, adjusted for multiple confounds. **Social isolation
  OR = 1.29; loneliness OR = 1.26; living alone OR = 1.32.** No
  difference between objective and subjective measures. Effects were
  *larger* in samples with mean age under 65 — the association is not
  only an old-age phenomenon.
- **Wang 2023, Nature Human Behaviour** — the independent, preregistered,
  much larger replication: **90 prospective cohorts, 2,205,199
  individuals.** All-cause mortality: **social isolation 1.32 (1.26–1.39);
  loneliness 1.14 (1.08–1.20).** Cancer mortality: isolation 1.24, loneliness
  1.09. CVD mortality: isolation 1.34. In people who already have CVD,
  isolation 1.28; in people with breast cancer, 1.51.
- **Steptoe 2013**, and this is the row that stops the block being
  simple. English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, **n = 6,500** aged 52+,
  mean follow-up **7.25 years**, Cox regression. Both isolation and
  loneliness looked associated with mortality unadjusted. **After
  adjusting for demographics and baseline health, social isolation stayed
  significant (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.08–1.48 for the top quintile) and
  loneliness did not (HR 0.92, 95% CI 0.78–1.09).** The authors'
  conclusion: efforts to reduce isolation are likelier to be relevant to
  mortality than efforts aimed at the feeling.

**The "as bad as 15 cigarettes a day" claim: NOT WHAT THE PAPER SAYS.**
I read the discussion of the open-access 2010 article directly. It says
the magnitude of the social-relationships effect is **comparable with
quitting smoking** and that it exceeds physical inactivity and obesity.
**It does not state an equivalence to any particular number of
cigarettes.** The 15-cigarettes formulation is a communication device
built downstream from a figure comparing effect sizes across risk
factors. **UNVERIFIED as a paper claim; do not print it.** The honest
sentence is the one the paper actually supports: the association is
about as large as the ones we already take seriously.

**The synthesis this gate wants recorded, because it resolves A1.**

Across these four papers plus Zagic 2022 in A1, two different things are
being measured and they behave differently.

| | measured how | what predicts mortality | what an intervention moves best |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Objective isolation** — who you actually see | contact counts, group membership, living alone | **Yes** — survives adjustment in Steptoe, and is the larger of the two in Wang 2023 | **Access to other people** (Zagic g = 0.67) |
| **Felt loneliness** — whether you feel known | UCLA and similar scales | Weaker; **did not survive adjustment** in ELSA | **Social-cognition skills** (Zagic g = −0.53; Masi's RCT moderator) |

So the friendship block is not inverted. It is two blocks. **Showing up
is what the mortality evidence is about. How you read the room is what
the loneliness-feeling evidence is about. Prescribing one for the other
is the error the corpus makes, in both directions.**

**Grade of the practice: C.**

The practice here is something like *keep at least one group you actually
turn up to, and notice when you have drifted* — which is what the live
`group-belonging` and `closeness-audit` cards do.

*Why C and not B.* Every one of these is observational. Not one randomised
anybody into more or fewer relationships and followed mortality — nobody
ever will. The exposure is measured by self-report; reverse causation
(illness causing withdrawal) is only partly controlled; residual
confounding by socioeconomic position is untestable at this scale; and
Steptoe shows the effect for the *felt* half disappears under adjustment
in the one large cohort that tested it properly. B means tested and it
held up. This was watched, not tested.

*Why C and not D.* Because the association is very large, very
consistent, and independently replicated at two million people with a
preregistered protocol. D would be dishonest in the other direction.

*Why not B on the "consistency across 90 cohorts" argument.* Consistency
across observational cohorts is consistency of design, not corroboration.
Ninety cohorts sharing the same confounding structure agree ninety times
about the same possible confound. The library's own rule — grade the
practice — settles it: nobody has shown that adding relationships to an
adult's life changes their mortality.

**No A, and this is the closest thing in the pillar to one.** The brief
predicted exactly that and the brief is right.

**Design, n and results**

- **Gable 2004**: four studies. Two showed that telling someone about a
  personal positive event was associated with higher daily positive
  affect and wellbeing **over and above the event itself and other daily
  events** — and that the benefit was larger when the listener was
  perceived to respond **actively and constructively** rather than
  passively or destructively. Two further studies linked having a partner
  who typically responds enthusiastically to higher intimacy and daily
  marital satisfaction. Diary and cross-sectional.
- **Reis 2010** is the one that lifts this out of correlational territory:
  **four experiments plus one daily diary study.** Sharing good news
  raised the perceived value of the event, especially when the other
  person responded enthusiastically, and enthusiastic responses built
  trust and prosocial orientation. Held **both with strangers and in
  everyday close relationships.**
- **Otto 2015**: dyadic intensive longitudinal, **99 couples**, 7–10
  consecutive days of electronic diaries, in a hard setting — women with
  early-stage breast cancer and their partners. On days a positive event
  was shared, both members felt more intimate; higher perceived partner
  responsiveness to the sharing predicted higher intimacy and lower
  negative affect for the sharer. **All effects held over and above event
  positivity and over and above sharing negative events** — that is the
  cleanest demonstration that this is not just "support".
- **The honest limits, both of which Gate 1 asked for.** Kashdan 2013:
  **174 heterosexual couples**, mean age 21.5, self-report, partner
  report and laboratory observation. Higher social anxiety predicted
  giving *and* receiving less supportive responses to shared good news,
  and inadequate capitalisation support predicted breakup and declining
  quality six months later — independently of depressive symptoms.
  Gable 2011: **101 dating couples**, videotaped and observer-coded.
  More avoidantly attached responders were rated less responsive,
  especially when their partner was anxiously attached, and avoidant
  people underestimated their partner's responsiveness relative to
  coders. **So this does not work identically for everyone, and the
  people who find it hardest are the ones the card would be aimed at.**

**Replication picture.** No meta-analysis of capitalisation was located.
Recent work continues to support it (a 2022 *J Soc Pers Relat* paper on
self-reported, perceived and observed responses; a 2025 *J Fam Psychol*
extension to postpartum couples), and none of it is a preregistered
direct replication. Nothing contradicts it that I found.

**Grade of the practice: B.**

The practice is: *when someone tells you something good, ask for the
detail and say why it lands.*

*Why this is one of the very few B grades in the round.* Reis 2010
experimentally manipulated the exact behaviour a card asks for — how
enthusiastically you respond to shared good news — and found the effect
in four experiments, in strangers and in close relationships, with a
diary study alongside. Otto 2015 replicated the association in a
different, harder population with a design that controls for the obvious
alternative explanations. The failure modes are documented, which is
usually a sign of a literature that has actually been worked on rather
than only celebrated. "Tested and it held up" is a fair description.

*Why not A, beyond the pillar's ceiling.* Even without the standing
constraint this would not be an A. No meta-analysis, no preregistered
replication, no long-term outcome trial, samples skewed to students and
dating couples, and outcomes measured in perceived intimacy rather than
anything durable. The A-grade practices in this library — morning light,
strength training — have dozens of trials with hard outcomes. This has
five good papers.

*The live `good-news-response` card is at C.* **Regrade to B.** See
section B.

**Papers opened**

| 28 | Kumar A, Epley N. *Undervaluing gratitude: expressers misunderstand the consequences of showing appreciation.* Psychological Science 2018 | 10.1177/0956797618772506 · PMID 29949445 | Kumar; Epley | `none`. 29 citations |
| 29 | Davis DE et al. *Thankful for the little things: a meta-analysis of gratitude interventions.* J Couns Psychol 2016 | 10.1037/cou0000107 · PMID 26575348 | — | `none`. 140 citations |

**Two corrections to Gate 1's bibliography, both material to attribution**

- Gate 1 recorded `10.1037/a0028723` as "Algoe, Gable & Maisel 2010 /
  Algoe et al. 2012". **It is Gordon, Impett, Kogan, Oveis & Keltner
  2012**, verified against the Crossref author list. **Amie Gordon is
  first author**, so an Amie Gordon researcher credit passes the
  author-list rule against this DOI. That is better news for Gate 1's
  recommendation than Gate 1 realised.
- Gate 1 recorded `10.1037/pspi0000178` as "Gordon et al. 2019". **It is
  Park, Impett, MacDonald & Lemay.** Amie Gordon is *not* on it. **A
  Gordon credit cited against this DOI would fail the author-list rule.**

**Results**

- **Gordon 2012**: multimethod — cross-sectional, daily-experience,
  observational and longitudinal. People who felt more appreciated were
  more appreciative in return; more appreciative people were more
  responsive to their partner's needs, more committed, and more likely
  still to be in the relationship later. Outside observers rated
  appreciative partners as more responsive and committed in laboratory
  interactions.
- **Park 2019**: five studies. Perceiving that a partner expresses
  gratitude buffered the usual negative association between attachment
  avoidance and satisfaction/commitment; the buffering ran on **perceived**
  rather than partner-self-reported expression. Their own internal
  meta-analysis suggests the benefit may be specific to buffering
  avoidance.
- **Kumar & Epley 2018**: **three experiments.** People wrote gratitude
  letters and predicted the recipient's reaction; recipients then reported
  it. Writers **underestimated how positive** recipients felt,
  **overestimated how awkward** it would be, and underestimated how
  surprised they would be about the reason. Expected awkwardness
  predicted unwillingness to express gratitude.
- **Davis 2016, and this is the limit nobody quotes**: gratitude
  interventions beat a measurement-only control on psychological
  wellbeing (**d = 0.31, 95% CI 0.04–0.58, k = 5**) but not on gratitude
  itself (d = 0.20, CI −0.04–0.44). Against an *alternative activity* the
  effects shrink: wellbeing **d = 0.17 (0.09–0.24, k = 20)**, and against
  an activity-matched comparison **d = 0.14 (0.01–0.27, k = 18)**.
  Against a **psychologically active** comparison, **d = −0.03
  (−0.13–0.07, k = 9)** — no better than another decent thing to do.

**Grade of the practice — "say one specific thing you noticed": C.**

*Why C and not B.* Gordon 2012 and Joel 2020 make appreciation one of the
best-attested correlates of relationship quality there is; Kumar & Epley
show experimentally that people systematically under-do it. But the
intervention meta-analysis says gratitude exercises are no better than
any other psychologically active practice, and none of these trials was
run in couples with a relationship outcome. The correlational half is
strong, the causal half is weak, and B needs both.

*Why C and not D.* Because the target belief — "they know, and saying it
would be awkward" — has been directly tested and is wrong. That is a real
finding a card can act on.

**The specificity claim (say exactly what they did, not just "thanks")
is UNVERIFIED.** Gate 1 flagged a "meta-analysis of 91 gratitude studies"
behind it (claim #105). Searched at Europe PMC and Crossref; **no
91-study gratitude meta-analysis was found.** The closest are Davis 2016
(k = 4–20) and a 2025 PNAS cross-cultural meta
(`10.1073/pnas.2425193122`). **Neither tests specific against general
gratitude.** Do not print the 91 figure or the specificity comparison as
evidence. Specificity remains a sane default and should be written as
one.

**Results**

**Results**

- **Furlong 2012 (Cochrane)** is the strongest single row in this pillar.
  **13 trials (10 RCTs, 3 quasi-randomised), 1,078 participants** (646
  intervention, 432 control). Child conduct problems fell: parent-reported
  **SMD −0.53 (95% CI −0.72 to −0.34)**, and — the part that matters —
  **independently assessed SMD −0.44 (−0.77 to −0.11)**. Parental mental
  health improved **SMD −0.36 (−0.52 to −0.20)**; positive parenting
  improved (parent-reported −0.53; independent −0.47); harsh parenting
  fell (parent-reported −0.77; independent −0.42). Cost roughly
  **US$2,500 per family** to move a child with clinical-level conduct
  problems into the non-clinical range. Authors' caveat: short term; the
  long-term picture needs more research.
- **Barlow 2016 (Cochrane), under-3s**, is much weaker and says so.
  **22 RCTs and 2 quasi-RCTs, 3,161 parents.** The headline result — total
  emotional and behavioural problems **SMD −0.81 (−1.37 to −0.25), 5
  studies, 280 participants, graded LOW quality** — **did not survive a
  sensitivity analysis removing the two quasi-RCTs (SMD −0.67, CI −1.43 to
  0.09)**. What holds at moderate quality is narrower: externalising
  problems **SMD −0.23 (−0.46 to −0.01), 8 studies, 989 participants**;
  observer-rated reductions in negative parent–child behaviour **−0.22**
  and increases in positive behaviour **0.48**. Every study is at high
  risk of performance bias because these interventions cannot be blinded.
  Authors call it "tentative support".
- **Thomas & Zimmer-Gembeck 2007**: 24 studies of PCIT and Triple P.
  Both reduce parent-reported child behaviour and parenting problems.
  Effects for PCIT were large on parent-report; **PCIT and an enhanced
  Triple P were the only variants associated with improvements in
  *observed* child behaviour**; Media Triple P had small effects.
- **The Triple P problem, which Gate 1 asked this gate to find, and it is
  worse than expected.** Sanders 2014 is a 101-study, 16,099-family
  meta-analysis reporting d = 0.473 for child outcomes and d = 0.578 for
  parenting — **and it is written by the programme's developers.**
  Wilson 2012 in *BMC Medicine*, independent and open access, took 33
  eligible Triple P evaluations and found: **31 of 33 compared Triple P
  only with waiting-list or no treatment**; most reported only maternal
  ratings; **maternally reported effect 0.61 (0.42–0.79)** but
  **paternally reported 0.42 (−0.02 to 0.87), not significant**; **the two
  studies with an active control group showed no between-group
  difference**; only five studies could compare beyond the end of the
  intervention; **32 of the 33 studies were authored by Triple P-affiliated
  personnel; no trials were registered; only two papers carried a
  conflict-of-interest statement**; and there was substantial selective
  reporting bias with preferential reporting of positives in abstracts.
  Their conclusion: no convincing evidence of population-level or
  long-term benefit.

**What this means for the library.** The library ships *components* —
child-led play, labelled praise, one-to-one time — not programmes. The
component evidence is thinner than the programme evidence, and after
Wilson 2012 the programme evidence itself is not uniform: **Cochrane's
independent reviews carry the weight; the developer meta-analyses do
not.**

| practice | grade | reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Ten minutes of play the child leads, describing rather than directing (`child-led-play`) | **C** | It is the first-taught component of the programmes Cochrane found effective, and PCIT is one of only two variants with *observed* child-behaviour improvement. Not D: the parent programme it comes from has independent RCT support at SMD −0.44 on blind-rated outcomes |
| Name the specific thing the child did (`labelled-praise`) | **C** | Same reasoning. Head-to-head specific-versus-generic praise trials remain small and short — I found none to change this |

### A11. Interparental conflict, not marital status — Gate 1's best new family finding, verified and trimmed

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 41 | van Dijk R, van der Valk IE, Deković M, Branje S. *A meta-analysis on interparental conflict, parenting, and child adjustment in divorced families: examining mediation using meta-analytic structural equation models.* Clinical Psychology Review 2020 | 10.1016/j.cpr.2020.101861 | `none`. 70 citations |
| 42 | Cummings EM, Davies PT. *Effects of marital conflict on children: recent advances and emerging themes in process-oriented research.* J Child Psychol Psychiatry 2002;43(1):31–63 | 10.1111/1469-7610.00003 · PMID 11848336 | `none`. 313 citations |
| 43 | Amato PR. *Children of divorce in the 1990s: an update of the Amato and Keith (1991) meta-analysis.* J Fam Psychol 2001;15(3):355–370 | 10.1037/0893-3200.15.3.355 | `none`; OpenAlex not retracted. **918 citations.** No abstract available at Crossref, Europe PMC or OpenAlex |

**Results**

- **van Dijk 2020** is the modern quantitative anchor: **2,257
  correlations across 115 samples, N = 24,854 divorced families**,
  three-level meta-analyses plus meta-analytic structural equation models.
  Findings: associations between interparental conflict, parenting and
  child psychosocial adjustment were mostly significant and in the
  expected direction, and **of small effect size**. Parental support,
  hostility, structuring, intrusiveness and role diffusion **mediated**
  the link between interparental conflict and children's internalising and
  externalising problems; dyadic parent–child processes did not. Negative
  parenting behaviours mattered more than positive ones, and **role
  diffusion** — pulling a child into the adult conflict — was singled out
  as a particular risk.
- **Cummings & Davies 2002** is the review that established the process
  framing: it is not conflict per se but its specific characteristics,
  and the child's own emotional and physiological response, that carry the
  effect.

**The correction Gate 1's claim needs.** Jefferson Fisher's on-air version
(#86) was that how parents handle conflict is *the strongest predictor* of
a child's wellbeing. **That is an overstatement.** van Dijk's effect sizes
are small, and Amato 2001 was not readable at any source available to
this gate — so the direct comparison against marital status is
**PARTLY VERIFIED at best**. What is verified is the mediation structure:
interparental conflict reaches children **through parenting**, and
role diffusion is the specific mechanism to avoid.

**Grade of the practice: C.**

The practice: *keep the disagreement between the two adults — no child
carrying messages, no child as confidant, no child asked to take a side.*

*Why C and not B.* Every input is correlational and the pooled effects
are small. No trial randomised co-parents to conflict containment.

*Why C and not D.* 115 samples and nearly 25,000 families, with a
consistent mediation structure and one named, specific, avoidable
mechanism. And the practice is *negative* — a thing to stop doing — which
is the safest kind of instruction to give at C.

*Why this is worth writing despite the small effects.* The card costs
nothing, it can be done by one parent alone regardless of what the other
does, and the alternative advice in circulation — stay together for the
children — is the thing the literature does not support.

  communication, emotional intimacy and partner behaviours, gains
  generally maintained. Greater baseline distress predicted larger gains.
  Limitations: exclusively opposite-sex couples.

**Verdict on the podcast claim.** Sue Johnson's stated recovery rate —
roughly three-quarters to seven-eighths of distressed couples — is a
developer-reported figure from her own trials and the developer-authored
Wiebe & Johnson review. **The independent meta-analysis restricted to
RCTs puts EFCT at g = 0.73 at post-test and g = 0.66 at six months, with
no advantage over behavioural couple therapy and an explicit publication-
bias warning.** That is a good, real, medium-to-large effect. It is not
the same claim.

**Grade: not a card.** This is a therapy, not a practice, and the library
does not ship therapy. It ships as a **safety route**: where a card's
safety line says a couples therapist is the right next step, this is the
evidence that the advice is worth taking. That is a better use of it than
a card would be.

**One derived practice does grade.** *Name the fear underneath the
complaint, once, instead of relitigating the incident* — Johnson's "reach
and respond". **Grade D.** It is the defining move of a treatment with
medium-to-large RCT support, but it has never been tested as a
self-administered practice, the trials are developer-led with publication
bias flagged, and the twelve-month picture for couple therapy generally is
poor. D, written warmly, with a clear route to a therapist.

### A15. Listening quality — the literature Duhigg gestures at, and it is better than he says

Gate 1 recorded Duhigg's practices as pointing at "communication research"
with no paper named (claims #35–43), and called them the strongest new
card candidates in the round. **The papers exist.** They are not Duhigg's
and they are not in the corpus.

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 49 | Itzchakov G, Weinstein N, Legate N, Amar M. *Can high quality listening predict lower speakers' prejudiced attitudes?* J Exp Soc Psychol 2020;91:104022 | 10.1016/j.jesp.2020.104022 | `none`. **Open access.** 12 citations |
| 50 | Itzchakov G, Weinstein N, Vinokur E, Yomtovian A et al. *Listening to understand: the role of high-quality listening on speakers' attitude depolarization during disagreements.* JPSP 2024 | 10.1037/pspa0000366 · PMID 37917499 | `none`. 9 citations |
| 51 | Itzchakov G, **Reis HT**, Weinstein N. *How to foster perceived partner responsiveness: high-quality listening is key.* Soc Personal Psychol Compass 2021;16(1) | 10.1111/spc3.12648 | `none`. Authors verified |

**Design, n and results**

- **Itzchakov 2020**: three studies, **two of them preregistered**, plus a
  pilot. Study 1 used scripted conversations at high, regular and poor
  listening quality; Study 2 manipulated listening quality in the lab
  while speakers talked about their own prejudiced attitudes; Study 3
  (preregistered) tested an alternative explanation. **An internal
  meta-analysis across the studies, N = 952**, gives high-quality
  listening versus comparison conditions: **self-insight d = 1.19 (95% CI
  0.73–1.51); openness to change d = 0.46 (0.29–0.63); prejudiced
  attitudes d = 0.32 (0.12–0.53).**
- **Itzchakov 2024, JPSP**: **four experiments** manipulating poor,
  moderate and high-quality listening — one by video vignette, **three in
  live interactions**. High-quality listening (attention, understanding,
  positive intentions) produced greater felt social comfort and connection
  and less defensive self-reflection, which in turn reduced both perceived
  and **actual** attitude polarisation. A serial mediation model was
  supported consistently and generalised across attitude certainty and
  attitude morality.

**What this does and does not license**

It licenses: *when you disagree with someone you love, listening properly
is the thing most likely to move them, and trying to counterargue is the
thing most likely not to.* That is Bill Doherty's prime directive (#100)
and Duhigg's why-question point (#43), and it now has four experiments
behind it rather than a workshop tradition.

It does **not** license Duhigg's "ten to twenty times more questions"
figure (#39) — **UNVERIFIED, no source found, do not print** — nor the
three-conversations taxonomy as a research finding. The taxonomy is a
useful frame from a trade book, not a tested construct.

**Grade of the practice: C.**

The practice: *in a disagreement that matters, listen to understand rather
than to answer — and stop trying to change their mind.*

*Why C and not B.* Four experiments with live interactions is real, but it
is essentially **one research programme** (Itzchakov, Weinstein, Reis,
Kluger and colleagues); the settings are laboratory conversations about
prejudice and politics rather than a kitchen argument with a sibling; the
downstream effect size on actual attitudes is modest (d = 0.32); and no
independent replication was located. B needs corroboration from outside
the originating group.

*Why C and not D.* Because the listening quality was **manipulated**, not
observed, in live conversations, with preregistration in two studies and
an internal meta-analysis of N = 952. That is a materially better evidence
base than anything the Gottman block offers.

**And the negative half is stronger than the positive half.** The
Boomerang finding — that counterarguing during someone else's turn
polarises rather than persuades — is the safest thing to write, because it
is an instruction to stop doing something.

### A16. Carers — well-studied, and the specific practices are not what was studied

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 52 | Sörensen S, Pinquart M, Duberstein P. *How effective are interventions with caregivers? An updated meta-analysis.* The Gerontologist 2002;42(3):356 | 10.1093/geront/42.3.356 | `none`. 634 citations |
| 53 | Pinquart M, Sörensen S. *Helping caregivers of persons with dementia: which interventions work and how large are their effects?* Int Psychogeriatr 2006 | 10.1017/S1041610206003462 | `none`. 483 citations |
| 54 | Belle SH et al. *Effect of multicomponent interventions on caregiver burden and depression: the REACH multisite initiative at 6-month follow-up.* Psychol Aging 2003;18(3):361 | 10.1037/0882-7974.18.3.361 | `none`. 222 citations |
| 55 | **Maayan N, Soares-Weiser K, Lee H.** *Respite care for people with dementia and their carers.* Cochrane 2014 | 10.1002/14651858.CD004396.pub3 | Crossref `update-to: new_version` of .pub2 — **an update, not a retraction.** 52 citations |
| 56 | Shaw C, McNamara R, Abrams K, et al. *Systematic review of respite care in the frail elderly.* Health Technology Assessment 2009;13(20) | 10.3310/hta13200 · PMID 19393135 | `none`. **Open access.** 78 citations |

**Results**

- **Sörensen 2002**: **78 caregiver intervention studies**, six outcomes.
  Combined interventions improved outcomes by **0.14 to 0.41 standard
  deviations** — burden, depression, subjective wellbeing, satisfaction,
  ability/knowledge and care-recipient symptoms. Effects were larger for
  ability and knowledge than for burden or depression. Psychoeducational
  and psychotherapeutic interventions were the most consistent.
  **Effects for dementia caregivers were smaller than for other groups.**
- **Pinquart & Sörensen 2006**: **127 studies**, 1982–2005. Significant
  but **small** effects on burden, depression, wellbeing, ability and care
  recipient symptoms. **Only multicomponent interventions reduced the risk
  of institutionalisation.** Psychoeducational interventions that require
  the caregiver to participate actively had the broadest effects; every
  other modality was domain-specific.
- **Belle 2003 (REACH)**: pooled 9 active against 6 control conditions
  across a multisite initiative at 6 months. Active interventions beat
  control on burden (p = .022), with larger benefits for women, for
  caregivers with lower education, for Hispanic caregivers and for
  non-spouses. Depression effects were site-specific.
- **The row that changes a live card. Cochrane 2014 on respite: four
  RCTs, 753 participants, evidence graded VERY LOW, and re-analysis found
  no significant effect of respite care compared with no respite care on
  any caregiver variable.** The authors are careful to say this reflects a
  lack of good research rather than a demonstrated lack of benefit.
- **HTA 2009** agrees and adds detail: 104 papers, 16 in meta-analysis.
  **Carer burden was reduced in single-sample studies but not in RCTs or
  quasi-experimental studies.** Depression was reduced in RCTs in the
  short term and for home care but not day care — **and those effects were
  not significant under random-effects models.** No effect on anxiety;
  positive effects on morale and on anger/hostility. Single-group studies
  suggested quality of life was *worse* after respite use. Their
  conclusion: some evidence of positive effect, but limited and weak.

**Grades**

| practice | grade | reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Two hours a week that are yours, with cover arranged** (`carer-own-hours`) | **D** — **regrade down from C** | The live `why` says a real break taken "lowers strain and low mood a little". **The Cochrane review of respite found no significant effect on any caregiver variable, and the HTA found the burden reduction present in single-sample studies and absent in RCTs.** That is precisely the design-dependent pattern Masi found in loneliness, and the live card is currently reporting the weak-design version. The practice is humane, cheap and probably right; the grade must be D and the `why` must be rewritten |
| **Ask one person for cover, once a week** (`carer-ask-for-cover`) | **D**, unchanged | Nothing tested this. The nearest evidence is that multicomponent, actively participatory psychoeducational programmes help a little and that domain-specific interventions do domain-specific things. Asking for practical help sits inside those packages and has never been isolated |
| **Ten minutes in the middle of the day that are not caring** (`carer-ten-minutes`) | **D**, unchanged | Borrowed from shift-work and burnout literature, as the card already says honestly |

**The important thing this gate wants said about the carer block.** The
evidence does not support promising a carer that a break will make them
feel better, and a card that promises it and then fails will land badly on
someone already at capacity. **What the evidence does support is that the
interventions which help are multicomponent, involve active
participation, and are delivered by services.** So the carer cards' honest
job is to be a route to those services as much as a practice — and
`carer-own-hours` already has a good safety line doing exactly that.
Strengthen it, keep `neverNag`, and drop the promise.

### A18. The finding that caps the whole couples block — and it is about *self-directed* practice, which is what this library ships

This is the second headline of the gate and Gate 1 did not have it. It is
uncomfortable and it belongs at the front of every couples card's
reasoning.

**Papers opened**

| # | citation | DOI / PMID | registry |
|---|---|---|---|
| 57 | Hawkins AJ, Blanchard VL, Baldwin SA, Fawcett EB. *Does marriage and relationship education work? A meta-analytic study.* J Consult Clin Psychol 2008;76(5):723–734 | 10.1037/a0012584 · PMID 18837590 | `none`. 133 citations |
| 58 | **McAllister S, Duncan SF, Hawkins AJ.** *Examining the early evidence for self-directed marriage and relationship education: a meta-analytic study.* Family Relations 2012;61(5):742–755 | 10.1111/j.1741-3729.2012.00736.x | `none`; OpenAlex not retracted. 34 citations |
| 59 | Hawkins AJ, Hokanson S, Loveridge E, et al. *How effective are ACF-funded couple relationship education programs? A meta-analytic study.* Family Process 2022;61(3):970–985 | 10.1111/famp.12739 | `none`. 16 citations |

**Results**

- **Hawkins 2008**, the optimistic baseline: 86 codable reports yielding
  **117 studies and more than 500 effect sizes.** For experimental
  studies, **relationship quality d = 0.30 to 0.36** and **communication
  skills d = 0.43 to 0.45.** Moderate-dosage programmes beat low-dosage
  programmes. Samples lacked racial, ethnic and economic diversity, and
  stability and aggression outcomes were rarely measured.
- **McAllister 2012 is the one that matters to this library.** It asks
  specifically what happens when the relationship education is
  **self-directed** — a book, a workbook, a set of exercises a couple does
  on their own, which is structurally what a protocol card is.
  **Sixteen self-directed studies: relationship quality d = 0.032,
  NOT SIGNIFICANT.** Communication skills **d = 0.160**, small but
  significant. **Thirteen blended studies (self-directed plus face-to-face)
  showed d = 0.429 for relationship quality and d = 0.715 for
  communication skills — larger than traditional face-to-face programmes.**
- **Hawkins 2022**, the largest recent control-group evidence, on
  publicly funded programmes for lower-income couples: **32 control-group
  studies.** Small but significant effects on relationship quality
  (**d = 0.114**), relationship skills (**d = 0.132**), mental health
  (**d = 0.074**) and coparenting (**d = 0.033**), and
  **non-significant effects on relationship stability, parenting and
  child wellbeing.** Nineteen single-group pre-post studies showed larger
  effects — the same design-dependency Masi found in loneliness and the
  HTA found in respite.

**What this means, said plainly**

Three independent things now point the same way:

1. **McAllister 2012**: self-directed relationship exercises do not
   reliably move relationship quality. They move communication skills a
   little.
2. **Lavner, Karney & Bradbury 2016** (A2c): the link from communication
   to satisfaction is not robust in either direction.
3. **Hawkins 2022**: even facilitated programmes shift relationship
   quality by about a tenth of a standard deviation and do not move
   stability, parenting or child outcomes at all.

**So the honest position for the couples block is this.** The practices
are cheap, kind, wanted by the people who open the app, and — done alone,
out of a book or an app — most likely to change how the two of you talk
rather than how the relationship goes. **What consistently does better is
adding a person: a facilitator, a course, a therapist.** The blended
effect sizes are three to twenty times the self-directed ones.

**This does not mean write nothing.** It means two concrete things.

- **Grades in the couples block are capped at C**, and most sit at D.
  Nothing here can be a B. The only B-graded practices in this pillar are
  the two that rest on their own experimental literatures and are not
  relationship-education exercises at all: `good-news-response` (A4) and
  `friend-unprompted-message` (A8).
- **Every couples card's route to a professional stops being a
  disclaimer and becomes part of the practice.** The evidence says the
  facilitator is the active ingredient. A coach that says so is being
  accurate, not defeatist — and per the README, "some of this is the
  ritual itself, and the ritual works" is an honest thing to say beside
  it.

## C. NEW CARD CANDIDATES

Fourteen, with the grade this gate would defend, the scheduling shape, and
the `neverNag` and safety requirements. **No `Protocol` objects are
written here** — that is Gate 4's job. Ordered by how much this gate would
argue for them.

Weighting against the brief: conflict repair 4, family and teenagers 3,
friendship and loneliness 3, carers 2, couples-and-money 1, transitions 1.
**The brief asked for 15–20 and this gate returns 14**, because three of
the areas it names — teenagers, blended families, sibling relationships in
adulthood — produced nothing this gate could verify (section G), and
padding them would be worse than the shortfall.

### C1. Before the thing, write what you expect them to think

**Practice.** Before a social event you are dreading, two minutes: write
what you expect the other people to think of you. Afterwards, one minute:
write what actually happened.

**Grade: C** (A1). Four meta-analyses agree that working on how a person
reads social situations beats supplying more contact for the *felt*
outcome; no trial has tested this two-minute self-administered version.

**Shape.** 3 minutes, `sessionType: journal`, twice — before and after —
anchored to the event rather than the clock. Fortnightly at most.
Frequency low on purpose: this is a belief-check, not a habit.

**`neverNag`: YES.** Someone avoiding social contact does not need a
streak broken.

**Safety.** Must distinguish ordinary social apprehension from social
anxiety that is shrinking someone's life, and route the second to a GP.
Must not read as "your loneliness is your own thinking error" — that is
the failure mode of this whole finding and it is the reason to write the
card carefully rather than not write it.

### C2. Listen to understand, not to answer

**Practice.** In the next disagreement that matters, take one turn where
your only job is to understand. No counterargument prepared while they
talk.

**Grade: C** (A15). Four experiments, three with live interactions,
manipulating listening quality; internal meta-analysis N = 952 with
d = 1.19 on self-insight and d = 0.32 on the downstream attitude.
One research programme, laboratory settings — hence C, not B.

**Shape.** 15 minutes, `energy: any`, no fixed day — it attaches to an
event. Best offered as a "next time" card rather than a scheduled one.

**`neverNag`: YES.**

**Safety.** Explicitly out of scope where someone is being coerced,
frightened or controlled: listening is not the answer to that, and a card
that says otherwise is the exact failure the brief forbids. Route to
1800RESPECT.

### C3. Their concern first, entirely

**Practice.** Once a week, when nothing is on fire, take one recurring
friction with a child and do it in order: ask what makes it hard *for
them*, and say nothing of your own until you can repeat theirs back. Then
your concern. Then, only then, what could we try.

**Grade: C** (A10). Ollendick 2016: RCT, n = 134, ages 7–14, CPS versus
parent management training versus waitlist; about 50 per cent of youth in
both active arms diagnosis-free at post-treatment against 0 per cent in
waitlist, maintained at 6 months. The card is one component of that
treatment, untested alone, in a non-clinical population.

**Shape.** 20 minutes, weekly, `anchor: fixed`, deliberately **not** at the
flashpoint hour — early evening on a calm day, or a weekend morning. The
"when it is calm, not in the moment" constraint is load-bearing and is the
scheduling shape.

**`neverNag`: YES.**

**Safety.** Say plainly that this is a way of talking, not a treatment,
and that a child whose behaviour is frightening them or the household
belongs with a GP or a properly delivered programme. Note that CPS and
structured parent training worked **equally well** — no parent should read
this as the one correct approach.

### C4. Keep it between the two of you

**Practice.** One thing to stop: no child carrying a message, no child as
confidant about the other parent, no child asked to agree with you.

**Grade: C** (A11). van Dijk 2020: 115 samples, N = 24,854 divorced
families; parenting mediates the effect of interparental conflict on
child adjustment, and **role diffusion** is named as a particular risk.
Effects are small and correlational.

**Shape.** Not a timed practice — a standing rule with a weekly 5-minute
review. `neverNag: YES`, `tier: should`.

**Safety.** Must not read as "keep the peace at any cost". Where a
child is being exposed to violence or to coercive control, containment is
not the instruction — 1800RESPECT and, where a child is at risk, the state
child-protection line. This is one of the cards most likely to be opened
by someone in a genuinely unsafe situation.

### C5. Say the feeling, not just the fact

**Practice.** Once this week, when you tell your partner about something
that happened, say the part about how it felt as well as the part about
what occurred.

**Grade: C** (A5). Laurenceau 1998 (two event-contingent diary studies)
and 2005 (96 couples, 42 consecutive days): disclosing emotion predicted
intimacy better than disclosing facts, with perceived partner
responsiveness as a partial mediator. Observational, never manipulated.

**Shape.** 5 minutes, 2–3 evenings a week, `energy: evening`, sleep- or
fixed-anchored. Pairs naturally with `partner-reunion`.

**`neverNag`: no** — this one is small enough that a missed day is
genuinely nothing.

**Safety.** None required beyond the pillar's standard.

### C6. Ask which one they want

**Practice.** When someone brings you a problem, ask before you answer:
do you want me to listen, or do you want help with it?

**Grade: D.** This is Gate 1's strongest new candidate (#36, #37, taught
in two independent Duhigg episodes) and **it has no direct trial.** The
adjacent evidence is the listening literature in A15, which shows that
listening quality — not solving — is what moves the other person. That
supports the *shape* of the question and not the question itself.
D, written warmly.

**Shape.** 2 minutes, no schedule — a "when it comes up" card. Best
delivered by the coach as a line to have ready rather than as a slot.

**`neverNag`: YES** (unschedulable).

**Safety.** None beyond standard.

### C7. Add a person

**Practice.** If you have been working on the relationship on your own for
three months and it has not shifted, book one session with a relationship
counsellor. Not because it is bad — because that is what the evidence says
works better.

**Grade: C** (A18, A12). McAllister 2012: self-directed relationship
education moves relationship quality d = 0.032 (ns), while **blended
self-directed plus facilitated programmes reach d = 0.429**. Rathgeber
2019: couple therapy RCTs, g = 0.60 overall at post-test, EFCT g = 0.73,
with publication-bias caution and no 12-month maintenance.

**Why this is the most unusual card in the round.** It is a card whose
content is that the cards are not the strongest thing available. That is
what the evidence says, the library's credibility rests on saying it, and
it is warm rather than defeatist: the coach handing someone a better
option is the coach doing its job.

**Shape.** 20 minutes, once, `anchor: deadline`. Australian routes named
without naming a product: Relationships Australia, a GP referral, or a
Medicare mental health treatment plan.

**`neverNag`: YES, absolutely.**

**Safety.** Must not imply failure. Must carry the family-violence route
separately, because couples counselling is contraindicated where there is
violence or coercive control, and that is a genuine safety point rather
than a disclaimer.

### C8. The recurring one, agreed

**Practice.** Take the argument you have had nine times. Agree, out of the
argument, how the two of you will have it — where, when, and how it ends.
Stop trying to finish it.

**Grade: D** (A2b). The observational description that most recurring
disagreements get managed rather than solved is well established in one
lab's coding work; the 69 per cent figure is untraceable and must not
appear; the practice has never been tested.

**Shape.** 25 minutes, monthly, `energy: any`, weekend daytime rather than
late evening.

**`neverNag`: YES.** **Safety:** couples-therapist route; family-violence
carve-out.

### C9. Name the fear under the complaint

**Practice.** Once, in the next disagreement, say the thing underneath —
what you are afraid of — rather than the thing that happened.

**Grade: D** (A12). It is the defining move of a therapy with medium-to-
large RCT support (EFCT g = 0.73 post, 0.66 at six months) but has never
been tested as something a person does alone, the trials are developer-led,
and publication bias is flagged.

**Shape.** 10 minutes rehearsal, `sessionType: journal`, then one real
sentence said to a real person. Fortnightly.

**`neverNag`: YES.**

**Safety.** Out of scope where there is fear of the partner — the same
carve-out `repair-rehearsal` already carries, and it must be worded
identically so the two cards do not contradict each other.

### C10. Money, once, together

**Practice.** One 30-minute conversation about how the two of you hold
money, and one decision at the end of it.

**Grade: C** (A13). Olson, Rick, Small & Finkel 2023: six-wave randomised
longitudinal experiment across the first two years of marriage; couples
assigned to merge finances sustained relationship quality while separate-
account and control couples declined. One experiment, one team, engaged
and newly married couples only, **n unconfirmed at this gate.**

**Shape.** 30 minutes, once, then annually. `goalDomains` should include
both `relationship` and money — **one card, two domains, per Gate 1's
cross-pillar note.**

**`neverNag`: YES.**

**Safety — the strongest requirement in this section.** Pooled finances
are a mechanism of financial abuse. The card must never frame separate
money as a failure, must say that keeping your own account is a legitimate
choice, and must carry 1800RESPECT. Money rules apply: name a licensed
professional as the next step, name no product, platform, ticker or
return.

### C11. What a break is actually for

**Practice.** The carer version of `carer-own-hours`, rewritten. Two hours
booked, cover arranged — and the honest reason: not that it will fix how
you feel, but that the carers services worry about are the ones who never
get one, and that the help with an evidence base behind it is a
multicomponent programme you have to be referred to.

**Grade: D** (A16). Cochrane 2014: no significant effect of respite on any
caregiver variable across 4 RCTs and 753 participants, very low quality.
HTA 2009: burden reduced in single-sample studies, not in RCTs.

**Shape.** 120 minutes, weekly, `anchor: fixed` with a wide window.
**`neverNag`: YES.**

**Safety.** Already good on the live card. Add the Australian carer route
(Carer Gateway, GP) and keep the "past tired" language, which is the best
sentence in the people file.

### C12. Ask for the thing you would not ask for

**Practice.** The existing `carer-ask-for-cover`, kept, at D, with an
**empty attribution** (section E).

**Grade: D**, unchanged.

### C13. The friend you assume does not want to hear from you

**Practice.** Not a new card — a **re-pointing of `friend-unprompted-message`
to be the friendship block's first rung** rather than one of eight
equivalent options.

**Grade: B**, unchanged (A8).

**Why it belongs in this section.** Gate 5 will need a friendship ladder,
and A1 says what its first rung should be: the belief about how a reach-out
will land, not the quota of people seen. This is the only card in the block
that does that, and it is also the best-evidenced one. **The ladder starts
here.**

### C14. Solitude counts

**Practice.** A framing card, not a scheduled one: some of the week being
alone is a need, not a failure of the friendship block.

**Grade: E**, and it should ship at E without embarrassment.

**Why include an E.** Because every other card in this block asks for
contact, and a library that only asks is one people leave. Kasley Killam
teaches this and traces cleanly (Gate 1 E1). Nothing measures it. E is the
honest grade and the README's expectancy section explicitly covers a
harmless practice written warmly at a low grade.

**`neverNag`: YES.** **Safety:** must distinguish chosen solitude from
withdrawal, and route persistent withdrawal alongside low mood to a GP.

## F. SAFETY

This pillar touches family violence, coercive control, estrangement and
bereavement. The brief's instruction is unambiguous: *if a practice could
be read as advice to stay and work on a relationship where someone is
unsafe, do not write it.* Everything below is written to that.

**Australian routes, to be used verbatim where a route is required:**

- **1800RESPECT — 1800 737 732** (family, domestic and sexual violence;
  24/7)
- **Lifeline — 13 11 14** (crisis support; 24/7)
- **13YARN — 13 92 76** (crisis support for Aboriginal and Torres Strait
  Islander people; 24/7)

Per the README, safety notes are written as care, not as disclaimer —
they should read like a good coach mentioning something.

### F1. The family-violence carve-out — which cards must carry it, and what it must say

**The test.** A card needs the carve-out if a reasonable reader who is
frightened of the other person could take it as an instruction to try
harder with that person.

**Cards that must carry it** (six live, five proposed):

| card | why |
|---|---|
| `repair-rehearsal` | Already has it, and its wording is the best in the library. **Use this card's sentence as the template for all the others** |
| `conflict-reappraisal-write` | Already has it. Consistent with the above |
| `turning-toward` | **Currently has NO safety field at all.** A card telling someone to answer every small approach from a partner they may be frightened of is the exact case the brief names. **Add** |
| `state-of-us` | Has a couples-therapist route; **needs the violence carve-out added**, because a scheduled conversation about the relationship is not safe to prescribe blind |
| `partner-checkin-weekly` | Same. Has the therapist route, needs the carve-out |
| `partner-appreciation` | **No safety field.** Lower risk than the above, but a card asking someone to voice appreciation daily toward a person who frightens them is not neutral. **Add a short version** |
| C2 *Listen to understand* | Listening is not the answer to coercion, and this card is the likeliest in the round to be misread that way |
| C4 *Keep it between the two of you* | Containment is the wrong instruction where a child is being exposed to violence |
| C7 *Add a person* | **Couples counselling is contraindicated where there is violence or coercive control.** This is a clinical fact, not a disclaimer, and the card must say it |
| C8 *The recurring one, agreed* | Same reasoning as `state-of-us` |
| C9 *Name the fear under the complaint* | Must use `repair-rehearsal`'s wording exactly, so the two cards cannot contradict each other |
| C10 *Money, once, together* | **The highest-risk card in the round.** See F2 |

**What the line must contain**, in this order: (1) the practice is out of
scope where there is fear, coercion or anything the person would call
abuse; (2) that needs a professional, not a practice; (3) the number.
It must **not** ask the reader to self-diagnose a category — "anything you
would call abuse" does the work, and it is already the live library's
wording.

### F2. The money card, specifically

`C10` rests on a randomised experiment showing that merging finances
sustained relationship quality. **Pooled finances are also a mechanism of
financial abuse and coercive control**, and a card that reads as
"merge your money" without saying so would be the most dangerous thing
this round could ship.

Three requirements, all mandatory:

1. The card frames the *conversation and the decision*, never the merge.
   Keeping your own account is written as a legitimate outcome of the
   conversation, not a failed one.
2. **Explicit line: if you do not feel free to say no to this
   conversation, or if money is being used to control what you can do,
   that is not a budgeting problem.** 1800RESPECT, 1800 737 732.
3. Money rules from the README: a licensed professional named as the next
   step, and no product, platform, ticker or return figure anywhere.

### F4. Bereavement, estrangement and separation

Three specific requirements the round must not lose:

- **Estrangement.** `adult-child-standing-call` and `closeness-audit` can
  both be opened by somebody estranged from the person in question. The
  standing-call card already has the right instinct — "a standing offer,
  not a standing demand" — and needs one more clause: **where someone has
  asked for no contact, that is the practice, and respecting it is not a
  failure of the card.**
- **Bereavement.** Nothing in this pillar should schedule contact with
  someone who has died out of a person's week. `family-memory-review` and
  `closeness-audit` are the two that could land on a recently bereaved
  person. Both need `neverNag` and a line that skipping is fine. **The
  library already names the five stages of grief as unsupported** — keep
  that standard and add nothing grief-shaped that is not evidenced.
- **Separation.** The brief names relationship transitions as `neverNag`
  territory and Gate 1 found a good separation candidate in James
  Sexton's observation that people rarely know which loss they are
  grieving. **This gate did not verify any literature behind it and does
  not recommend carding it this round.** Recorded so the next round can
  pick it up rather than lose it.

### F5. Two things this gate will not write

- **Anything that tells a parent their working arrangements are harming
  their child.** A17 records why. The claim is not supported by the
  literatures it invokes, and the brief forbids anything implying somebody
  is failing at their family.
- **Any card that reads as a test of whether a relationship will last.**
  A2a. Not at any grade, not with any hedge.

### G3. A tooling note for the next round

Three things cost real time at this gate and are worth writing down.

1. **`pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov` returns a CAPTCHA to automated fetches** on
   article and table URLs, intermittently. The `europepmc.org` REST
   `fullTextXML` endpoint returned an empty document for the same PMCID.
   The route that worked was a browser-agent fetch of the PMC article page
   through `WebFetch` — which succeeded for Heyman & Smith Slep and for
   the Masi article body, and failed for the Masi table URL.
2. **`SOURCES.md`'s OpenAlex fallback is doing exactly the job it was
   added for.** Three load-bearing papers in this round — Gottman &
   Levenson 2000, Gottman 1993 and Amato 2001 — have no abstract at
   Crossref or Europe PMC. OpenAlex rescued the first two. The third is
   closed everywhere, and that is worth knowing before anyone builds on
   Amato.
3. **The Crossref `update-to` field fired three times in this round and
   was benign every time** — Cochrane `CD008225.pub2`, `CD003680.pub3` and
   `CD004396.pub3` all carry `type: new_version`, meaning they supersede
   an earlier version of the same review. `RETRACTIONS.md` is right that
   the relation fields are unreliable for retractions; it is worth adding
   that they are also **noisy in the other direction** for Cochrane, and a
   round that treats every non-empty `update-to` as a red flag will stop
   three times for nothing. The discriminator is the `type` value.

## CLOSING — what this gate hands downstream

**The integrity test holds. No A is proposed, and the ceiling was not
approached.** The pillar's three B grades are `child-bedtime-routine`
(untouched this round), `friend-unprompted-message` (confirmed) and
`good-news-response` (regraded up) — and the two this gate examined rest on
experimental literatures that are not about couple or family behaviour at
all: one is about a belief people hold about being in touch, the other
about how a listener responds to good news. **Nothing about how two people
run a relationship earned a B, which is exactly what
`BRIEF-relationships.md` predicted.**

**Distribution after this gate: A 0 · B 3 · C 10 · D 17 · E 1.** More D
than before. That is the honest direction of travel for a gate that read
McAllister 2012 and the Cochrane respite review.

**The three things Gate 3, 4 and 5 must not lose.**

1. **The friendship ladder starts with the belief, not the quota.**
   A1 and A3 together: showing up is what the mortality evidence is about;
   how you expect to be received is what the loneliness-feeling evidence
   is about. `friend-unprompted-message` is rung one and it is a B.
   Contact quotas are not rung one and the numbers behind them are a
   book's mnemonic.
2. **The couples block's honest ceiling is set by McAllister 2012**, and
   the coach can say the reason out loud: doing this alone changes how you
   talk more than how it goes, and adding a person is what the evidence
   actually supports. That is a warm thing to be able to say, and no
   competitor in this category says it.
3. **Seven cards carry a researcher credit that fails the author-list
   rule**, and E4/E5 gives the exact DOIs that fix two of them. That is a
   one-lookup-each job and it is the cheapest credibility win available.

**And the tone, which the brief cares about more than the grades.** The
best-supported things in this pillar are still small, cheap and
unglamorous: answer the good news properly, send the message you nearly
did not send, listen without getting your answer ready, ask what makes it
hard for them before you say what makes it hard for you. The evidence is
modest and the practices are decent. **Nothing this gate found is a reason
to tell anybody they are failing at their family**, and several things it
found are reasons to tell somebody they can stop measuring one.
