# Sleep: verified source ledger (sleep debt, naps, alcohol, CBT-I, regularity/duration, extension, overclaims)

Compiled 2026-09-08. Verification method: every row's landing page was fetched and title / first author / year / journal / design / n confirmed against it. PubMed served a cookie wall, so the Europe PMC REST record for the same PMID (a mirror of the PubMed record) was used as the equivalent landing page, supplemented by PMC full text or the publisher page where the abstract lacked n. Wiley and PubPeer returned 403; those rows were verified via Europe PMC + Crossref instead. Rows marked UNVERIFIED could not be opened. "Why We Sleep" was not used as a source; where a claim traces to it, primary literature was checked (section 7).

Grading: A = meta-analyses and broad agreement; B = controlled trials or very large consistent cohorts; C = smaller, narrower or mixed studies; D = practitioner practice ahead of research; E = unproven. Grade is for the study, not the speaker.

**Synthesis, section 1.** What recovers quickly: subjective sleepiness (Van Dongen 2003 plateau; Rupp 2009 no group difference) and, given enough nights, PVT vigilance. What does not recover fully in the timeframes tested: PVT performance after chronic restriction (Belenky 2003: no recovery after 3 x 8 h nights in the 5 h/7 h groups; Banks 2010: deficits remain after one 10 h night), and insulin sensitivity under a repeating weekday-restriction / weekend-recovery cycle (Depner 2019). Recovery nights used: Van Dongen 3; Belenky 3 (8 h); Banks 1 (0–10 h); Rupp 5 (8 h); Kitamura 9 (12 h; ~4 days to clear 1 h/night of debt). Honest reading: modest debt (~1 h/night) clears over several nights of extended opportunity, not one weekend; larger chronic debt shows adaptation that masks impairment. Åkerstedt 2019 is consistent with weekend catch-up being *not harmful* and possibly compensatory for mortality, which is a different claim from "catch-up repays the metabolic debt".

**Synthesis, section 2.** Duration: 10 min of actual sleep is the best-evidenced short nap (Brooks & Lack 2006); 20 min works but with a ~35 min delay to benefit; 30 min or more brings sleep inertia and should be finished 30+ min before anything demanding. Timing: early afternoon (Lovato & Lack 2010; Dutheil 2021's before-13:00 subgroup). Who it helps: sleep-restricted, shift-working and young adults in lab conditions; habitual nappers more than novices (Milner & Cote 2009). Who it may harm or where caution applies: people with insomnia (stimulus control and sleep restriction in CBT-I both instruct no daytime napping, see section 4); older adults with long (≥60 min) daily naps, where long naps mark risk (Yamada 2015) even if they may not cause it. Whether occasional naps protect the heart (Häusler 2019) is a single-cohort finding and unproven.

**Synthesis, section 3.** Dose: REM suppression begins at roughly two standard drinks (Gardiner 2025), so "one glass" is near the threshold rather than clearly safe; at ~1 drink the measurable effect is autonomic (Pietilä 2018: ~9 percentage-point drop in HRV recovery at ≤0.25 g/kg), with sleep architecture effects small and uncertain. The sedative, faster-onset, more-SWS first half is real but only clearly at high doses, and is paid for with second-half fragmentation (Ebrahim 2013; Gardiner 2025). How long before bed: the only controlled timing study (Landolt 1996) shows a moderate dose taken 6 h before bed, fully metabolised by lights-out, still doubled second-half wakefulness; there is no controlled evidence that a particular "hours before bed" cut-off removes the effect. Practical rule that the evidence supports: fewer drinks matters more than earlier drinks.

**Component evidence summary (from Edinger 2021 review + van Straten 2018 + Maurer 2021):** multicomponent CBT-I: strong. Sleep restriction: large effects alone (g ≈ 0.9 on ISI/SE), no TST gain, transient vigilance cost in weeks 1–2. Stimulus control: conditional-for; effective alone in older trials but the standalone base is thinner than SRT's. Cognitive strategies: little standalone evidence; carried inside multicomponent CBT-I. Relaxation: conditional-for, smaller effects than SRT/stimulus control. Sleep hygiene alone: conditional-against; weaker than CBT-I in every head-to-head.

**On confounding of long-sleep associations (say it plainly).** Every long-sleep mortality figure above comes from self-reported duration measured once, years before death. Long sleep is a marker of depression, undiagnosed OSA, inflammatory illness, unemployment, low activity and frailty; the AASM/SRS panel therefore left >9 h as "uncertain" rather than harmful, and Jike 2018 closes by asking whether the relationship is causal at all. No trial has ever lengthened sleep and produced harm. Treat "long sleep shortens life" as an unproven reading of a confounded association (E as a causal claim), while "short sleep is associated with modestly higher mortality" is A as an association with a small effect (RR ~1.06–1.12).

## Contradicted practices

1. **Sleep hygiene as the treatment for chronic insomnia** — contradicted by AASM 2021 (conditional against), Chung 2018 and Espie 2019 (it was the losing control arm).
2. **"Sleep in at the weekend to repay the week"** as a metabolic strategy — contradicted by Depner 2019; the performance literature (Belenky, Banks) shows 1–3 recovery nights are insufficient after a week of real restriction.
3. **Nightcap for sleep** — contradicted by Gardiner 2025, Ebrahim 2013, Pietilä 2018, Landolt 1996.
4. **Long daily naps (≥60 min) as routine in older adults** — associated with higher CVD and mortality (Yamada 2015); at minimum a flag for undiagnosed night-sleep or health problems.
5. **Napping while treating insomnia** — contradicted by stimulus control and sleep restriction protocols within CBT-I.
6. **Chasing exactly 8 hours** — no consensus body recommends it; need varies ~7.3–9.3 h in one small lab sample and the consensus range is 7–9 h.
7. **Trusting how sleepy you feel as a gauge of debt** — subjective sleepiness plateaus while performance keeps falling (Van Dongen 2003; Rupp 2009).

## Practices the evidence supports, with scheduling shape

| Practice | Evidence base | Grade | Scheduling shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim for ≥7 h of actual sleep most nights, individually calibrated | Watson 2015, Hirshkowitz 2015 (consensus); Cappuccio/Shen/Yin (A associations); Kitamura 2016 for individual variation | A (consensus) / B–C (underlying) | Estimate personal need by 1–2 weeks of unrestricted sleep opportunity and the size of the first-night rebound (Kitamura); then set bedtime to give that duration plus ~30–45 min of bed time for normal efficiency |
| Keep sleep timing regular, seven days a week | Windred 2024 (n≈61k, SRI beat duration); Depner 2019 (weekend shift delayed circadian phase) | B | Same wake time ±30–60 min daily; if catch-up is needed, add it as an earlier bedtime or a short nap rather than a late lie-in |
| Repay debt over multiple nights, not one | Kitamura 2016 (~4 nights per 1 h/night debt); Banks 2010; Belenky 2003; Rupp 2009 | B/C | After a short week: 3–5 nights of +60–90 min opportunity; expect alertness to return first and performance/metabolic measures to lag |
| Bank sleep before a known restriction | Rupp 2009 | C | 5–7 nights of extended opportunity (the trial used 10 h TIB) before a known heavy period; modest, but measurable on vigilance |
| Short afternoon nap when sleep-restricted | Brooks & Lack 2006; Lovato & Lack 2010; Dutheil 2021 | C | 10 min of sleep (set ~15–20 min in bed), between ~13:00 and 15:00; if ≥30 min is taken, allow 30+ min for inertia to clear; avoid entirely if being treated for insomnia; avoid after ~16:00 |
| Athletes: extend sleep in blocks | Mah 2011; Cunha 2023 | C | >3 consecutive nights, target 9–10 h TIB, during training blocks; treat as promising, not proven |
| Alcohol: fewer drinks matters more than earlier drinks | Gardiner 2025; Pietilä 2018; Landolt 1996 | A (dose) / C (timing) | Keep intake ≤1 standard drink on nights when sleep quality matters; do not rely on a "3 hours before bed" cut-off, since a moderate dose 6 h before bed still fragmented the second half |
| Chronic insomnia: CBT-I (digital acceptable), sleep restriction first if only one component is feasible | Edinger 2021; van Straten 2018; Trauer 2015; Espie 2019; Maurer 2021 | A | 4+ sessions over 6–8 weeks; SRT: fixed wake time, TIB set to average sleep time (floor ~5–5.5 h), titrate up by 15 min when efficiency >85–90%; warn about reduced vigilance in weeks 1–2 (Maurer *JSR* 2021) and avoid safety-critical driving/operating during titration |
| Do not use subjective sleepiness as the sole gauge | Van Dongen 2003; Rupp 2009 | B | If a role is safety-critical, track hours of opportunity, not felt alertness |
