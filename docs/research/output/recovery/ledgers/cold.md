# Cold Water Immersion / Deliberate Cold Exposure — Verified Source Ledger

Compiled 2026-09-08. Verification method: every paper below was checked against a machine-readable primary record (Europe PMC REST record of the PubMed abstract, PMC full text, publisher page, or Crossref DOI metadata). Wiley, APS (journals.physiology.org), Springer and PubMed HTML pages returned 403/cookie walls, so the Europe PMC mirror of the PubMed abstract was used as the landing-page equivalent; DOI/PMID are given for each. Anything not opened is marked UNVERIFIED. Podcasts were not used as sources.

Grading: A = meta-analyses and broad agreement; B = controlled trials or very large consistent cohorts; C = smaller, narrower or mixed studies; D = what experienced people do ahead of research; E = unproven.

## 3. Contradicted practices

1. Routine ice bath immediately after every resistance session in a hypertrophy or strength block — contradicted by Roberts 2015, Fyfe 2019, Fuchs 2020 and every pooled analysis (Malta 2021, Grgic 2023, Piñero 2024, Yu 2026). Direction consistent, size small-to-moderate (hypertrophy cSMD −0.22; strength SMD −0.23 to −0.60).
2. Using CWI to "reduce muscle inflammation" after lifting — Peake 2017 found no difference vs active recovery in muscle; Cain 2025 shows systemic inflammatory markers rise acutely.
3. Cold exposure as a fat-loss tool — no immersion trial shows fat loss; the best air-exposure trial shows 0.7 kg over 6 weeks at 2 h/day.
4. Cold as an immune booster — pooled RCT effect null; the one large trial reduced absence, not illness.
5. Breath-hold submersion / dunking the head in very cold water, especially with cardiovascular disease — Shattock & Tipton 2012 (autonomic conflict), Tipton 1989/2017.
6. Long or "push through" immersions for novices in open water — cold incapacitation after ~3 min and swim failure well before hypothermia (Knechtle 2020; Tipton 1989).
7. Contrast water therapy as superior to plain CWI — Bieuzen 2013 and Higgins 2017 do not support superiority.

## 4. What the evidence supports, with scheduling shape

Each item carries its grade.

1. Short cold immersion improves mood/alertness acutely (C). Shape: 5 min head-out at ~14–20 °C (Yankouskaya 20 °C × 5 min; Kelly & Bird 13.6 °C up to 20 min). Expect a within-hours lift in positive affect and a fall in tension/fatigue scores; no evidence of a sustained day-long effect. Time of day: no trial has compared morning vs evening; the alertness/catecholamine profile makes morning or pre-work use the plausible default (D). Avoid within a few hours of bedtime on the same physiological logic (D; untested).
2. Daily hot-to-cold shower ending 30–90 s reduces self-reported sickness absence but not days ill (B). Shape: 30 consecutive days; 30 s performed the same as 60 or 90 s (p=0.98 between groups), so the shortest dose is the evidence-based one. Do not present as an immune effect.
3. Cold immersion 10–15 min at 10–15 °C reduces soreness at 24–96 h and modestly improves next-day power after eccentric or high-intensity work (A). Shape: use after competition, tournament days or hard conditioning sessions when performance the next day matters more than adaptation.
4. Keep cold away from resistance training when muscle growth or strength is the goal (A for the interference; D for the exact interval). The harmful trials applied CWI within minutes of training. No trial has tested a "safe" delay; the 2026 Exp Physiol review proposes >8 h. Practical shape: cold on non-lifting days, or in the morning of a lifting day with the session in the evening, or omit CWI during hypertrophy blocks. Whole-body immersion appears less harmful to strength than limb-only immersion (Grgic 2023), but hypertrophy data do not show that reassurance.
5. Endurance training tolerates post-session cold (B/A): 6 weeks of SIT with CWI produced the same VO2max, mitochondrial respiration and 2-km time-trial gains as control (Broatch 2017); pooled endurance outcomes neutral (Malta 2021). Cold does not add endurance adaptation either.
6. Stress reduction is time-dependent and uncertain (C): pooled effect is null immediately, 1 h, 24 h and 48 h, and appears only at 12 h (SMD −1.00) from few studies. Don't promise "instant calm"; the acute response is a sympathetic surge.
7. Safety shape (A for physiology; D for practice rules):
   - Enter gradually; the cold-shock gasp/hyperventilation peaks at ~30 s and settles over ~2–3 min. Never combine sudden immersion with breath-holding; keep the head out.
   - Novices: minutes, not tens of minutes, at ≤15 °C; get out before manual dexterity or swimming ability fails (incapacitation begins after ~3 min in very cold water).
   - Hypothermia is a >30 min problem for healthy adults, but swim failure and drowning come first; never alone in open water.
   - Afterdrop of ~0.3–0.7 °C occurs after exiting, more with movement and passive rewarming; dress in dry insulating layers and rewarm actively but gently.
   - Do not start cold immersion with known or suspected cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia or long-QT; pregnancy: no data, expert consensus advises against starting as a novice; Raynaud's: expert lists only, no trial data.
   - For healthy, habituated, head-out dippers the measured arrhythmia burden during ~7 °C immersion was not significantly above baseline (Merkt 2026, n=20) — reassurance that applies only to that profile.
8. Contrast (hot/cold water) therapy for soreness (B): better than passive rest, not better than cold alone; sauna-then-plunge has no controlled outcome evidence (E) and remains a D-grade practice.
