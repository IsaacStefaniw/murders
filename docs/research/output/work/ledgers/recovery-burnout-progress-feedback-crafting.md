# Work round — recovery from work, burnout, progress principle, feedback, autonomy & job crafting

Research ledger for IntentNorth (Work & Leadership coach). Written incrementally, 7–8 Sep 2026.

**Verification method.** Publisher landing pages return 403 from this environment, so every row was checked at record level against (a) the Crossref API record for the DOI (title, authors, journal, volume, pages, year, and the `update-to` / `relation` fields that carry retraction and correction notices), and (b) the Europe PMC REST record (PMID, abstract text, and the numbers stated in it). Where a paper is not indexed in PubMed (most management journals), the abstract came from Crossref where it carries one, or from Semantic Scholar / the author's institutional page. Status per row:

- **VERIFIED** — bibliographic record confirmed and every number in the "key result" column read from the abstract or full text at a registry/PMC/author source.
- **PARTLY VERIFIED** — bibliographic record confirmed; some numbers come from the abstract, others (flagged `[unverified]`) from memory or secondary sources and could not be read on a primary record.
- **UNVERIFIED** — could not confirm the record, or the numbers, at any primary source. Treat as a lead, not a citation.

**Existing library entries this ledger touches** (do not duplicate; regrade or deepen): `detachment-window` (mind, B), `shutdown-ritual` (mind, D), `load-and-control-review` (mind, C), `accomplishment-log` (leadership, D), `one-on-one-cadence` (leadership, C, already cites Kluger & DeNisi), `deep-work` (leadership, D), `meeting-free-morning`/`message-batching`/`meeting-trim` in protocols.work.ts.

**Grades.** A meta-analyses/broad agreement · B controlled trials or very large consistent cohorts · C smaller/narrower/mixed · D experienced-practitioner practice ahead of research · E unproven. The grade is for the study, never the speaker.

**Audience note.** Roughly half of IntentNorth's users are not at a desk. Every section closes with a line on whether the evidence comes from knowledge workers only and what exists for shift, physical and frontline work.

**A caution about the grade spread, because the brief asks for it.** Sections 1–5 come back **A 10 · B 16 · C 14 · D 3 · E 1**, against a current leadership pillar of A 2 · B 4 · C 5 · D 9 · E 3. That looks like grade inflation and it is not. **These are grades for studies, not for practices.** Ten genuine meta-analyses sit in this slice — Bennett, Wendsche, Steed, Panagioti, Maricuțoiu, Kluger & DeNisi, Wisniewski, Scullen, Rudolph, Humphrey — several with tens of thousands of participants, and grading them below A would be dishonest in the other direction. **The practices those studies support grade much lower**, because in almost every case the A-grade finding is an *association* or a *negative* result rather than a tested intervention. Section (a) below carries the practice-level grades, and they run B/C/D — one B raise (`shutdown-ritual` D→C is the only regrade this slice proposes upward, plus holding `detachment-window` at B on much firmer footing). Read the two as separate columns; conflating them is exactly the failure the brief warns about.

**Audience note for section 1.** This literature is *mostly* knowledge workers — public administration (Sonnentag 2008), German office staff (Hahn 2011), employer-phone users (Derks 2014). Three exceptions carry the non-desk case and all three are graded C: **emergency service workers** (Fritz & Sonnentag 2005, n=87), **human service employees** (Sonnentag 2010, n=309), and **hospital nurses** (Cho 2025, n=1,115). No detachment study located on tradespeople, drivers, chefs, or construction. The meta-analyses (Bennett, Wendsche, Steed) pool across occupations but do not report occupation as a moderator in their abstracts, so the honest position is: **the construct is demonstrated in frontline health and emergency work; it is untested in trades, transport and hospitality.**

## 2. Burnout — the construct, and what interventions actually do

**Read this section's bottom line first:** every controlled meta-analysis here finds individual-level burnout interventions produce **small** effects, and the two that compared intervention *types* head-to-head both found **organisation-directed interventions roughly two to two-and-a-half times more effective** than person-directed ones. The app must not imply a person can breathe their way out of an unsustainable job.

**Replication status of the progress principle, stated plainly.** I could not locate an **independent** diary study that set out to test and confirm the progress principle as such. Searches for daily-diary replications returned adjacent work (daily goal progress and work engagement, remote-work goal progress) but nothing framed as a direct replication with the original's design. The general claim underneath it — that daily progress on meaningful work predicts positive affect at work — is compatible with a large within-person diary literature, but **"progress is the single strongest driver of inner work life" is a claim from one research group's proprietary dataset, published in a book and a magazine.** Treat the *practice* (notice and record what you actually finished) as cheap, harmless and well liked; treat the *ranking* claim as unreplicated.

**Audience note for section 3.** Entirely knowledge workers, and the authors say so themselves — HBR's own abstract calls them "knowledge workers", and the ASQ sample is professional project teams in seven companies. **Nothing here was tested on shift, physical or frontline work.** The plausible transfer is that a nurse, chef or tradesperson finishes visible, discrete units of work every shift and therefore has *more* natural progress signal than a knowledge worker on a nine-month project — but that is a reasonable guess, not a finding, and should be written as such.

# (b) Overclaims and time-back

Roughly a fifth of the round, as the contract asks. Framed as minutes returned, not as corrections.

**1. The feedback sandwich — you can stop constructing them.**
The entire empirical literature is about three studies. Parkes et al. 2013 (N=20 and N=350 medical students) found sandwiches **changed what people thought about the feedback and not what they did with it** — and named the cost: the effort of building them. Prochazka et al. 2020 found a positive effect in **91 students on a maths test**, and the authors themselves could not tell whether the benefit came from the sandwich structure or **merely from there being something positive in the message at all**. Meanwhile Kluger & DeNisi's 607 effect sizes show that feedback goes wrong when attention moves **from the task to the self**, and wrapping a criticism in unrelated praise is a technique for making a conversation more about the person. **Time back: the minutes spent engineering the bread.** What to do instead is not "be blunt" — it is to keep every sentence about the work.

**2. Burnout is not something a person can fix on their own, and the app should never imply it is.**
Panagioti 2017: organisation-directed interventions **SMD −0.45**, physician-directed **−0.18**. Bes 2023: workload-focused organisational interventions **−0.44**, and the best results came from **combined** organisational + individual (**−0.54**). Maricuțoiu 2016: across all employees, controlled interventions moved **exhaustion by d = .17** and moved **depersonalization (d = .04) and personal accomplishment (d = −.02) not at all**. Ahola 2017, in people who **already have** burnout: four pooled RCTs of individual interventions showed **no effect on exhaustion or cynicism**, and there were not enough studies to pool return-to-work at all. **Time back: any programme sold as personally curing burnout.** The honest coach line is that the six-area check exists to help you *name* which part of the job is the problem and *who to say it to* — that is the lever the evidence supports.

**3. "Physician burnout doubles the risk of patient safety incidents" — retracted.**
Panagioti et al. 2018, *JAMA Internal Medicine* 178(10):1317–1331, **retracted 2020** after a University of Manchester panel found flaws in the systematic review process and could not confirm the results were valid. It is still cited 824 times. If it appears in a keynote, a course or a podcast, it is not evidence. **Time back: the anxiety.**

**4. The 3:1 positivity ratio.**
Fredrickson & Losada's 2005 *American Psychologist* paper — the source of "you need three positives for every negative" in feedback and relationship advice — carries a **formal correction (10.1037/a0034435)** following Brown, Sokal & Friedman's 2013 critique, *"The complex dynamics of wishful thinking: the critical positivity ratio"* (10.1037/a0032850). Fredrickson's own reply, "Updated thinking on positivity ratios" (10.1037/a0033584), conceded the mathematical modelling. Crossref records the correction against the original with `type: "correction"` from both the publisher and Retraction Watch. **Time back: counting your compliments to a ratio that was never real.**

**5. "Recovery is about doing relaxing things."**
Bennett 2018 found **control** — having a say over your own off-hours — predicted *vigor* better than either detachment or relaxation. Wendsche 2017 found detachment had **no significant relationship with physiological stress markers at all**, and a **negative** relationship with creativity and contextual performance. Steed 2021 found that recovery *activities*, *experiences* and *states* are **only modestly related to each other** — so "I did a relaxing thing" is genuinely not the same variable as "I feel recovered". **Time back: prescribed relaxing activities somebody does not choose.** An evening the person controls beats an evening the app designs.

**6. The progress principle's ranking claim is unreplicated.**
That progress is *the single most powerful driver* of inner work life comes from **one research group's proprietary diary corpus**, published in a book and in *Harvard Business Review*, with no effect sizes, no significance tests and no independent re-analysis. The practice — notice what you finished — is cheap, harmless and worth doing. **The ranking is not a finding you can lean on**, and a D grade on the log is the honest one. Note that Amabile & Kramer's own HBR abstract says **praise without real progress "could even arouse cynicism"** — which is a genuinely useful and much less quoted part of their work.
