# Brief: the Money coach

Read `docs/research/README.md` first.

## Current state

11 protocols — A 1 · B 4 · C 3 · D 1 · E 2. **Joint-thinnest pillar in the
library**, and it carries a full pathway plus the goal ladder that turns
"save $100k" into dated milestones with a weekly number.

Isaac's own assessment: the money coach needs work. This round is a rebuild
more than a top-up.

## Hard constraints, before anything else

Enforced by test, no exceptions:

- **Every protocol touching debt, investing or a position number must name
  a licensed professional as the next step.**
- **No protocol may name a product, platform, ticker or return figure.**
  Not one. No index funds by name, no platforms, no historical returns, no
  "7% a year".

This is what keeps the pillar on the education side of the line, and in
Australia it is what keeps it clear of financial advice regulation. Work
inside it, and if a practice cannot be written without a return figure,
that is a finding for `findings.md`, not a protocol.

## The audience is Australian

This matters more here than in any other pillar, because money practice is
jurisdictional in a way that sleep is not. Superannuation, the tax year
ending 30 June, offset accounts, HECS-HELP, the first home schemes,
Centrelink — none of this maps onto American personal-finance content, and
most of the available material is American.

**ASIC's MoneySmart is the reference point**: government, independent,
free of product interest, and written for exactly this audience. The ATO
is the authority on anything tax-shaped. Use them heavily.

Where you use international behavioural research — and you should, it is
where the good evidence is — check whether the mechanism survives
translation. Automatic escalation of retirement contributions is a strong
finding; how it maps onto salary sacrifice into super is a different
question.

## Where the depth is missing

**Behavioural mechanisms with real trial evidence.** This is where the
genuine A and B grades live in this pillar:

- Automatic escalation and pre-commitment (Save More Tomorrow is a real
  field experiment with real effect sizes)
- Default effects on enrolment and contribution
- Future-self continuity and how it changes saving behaviour
- Mental accounting and the separate-account effect
- Implementation intentions applied to money, which connects to machinery
  the app already has in the when-then lines

**The savings-rate engine needs evidence behind it.** The app computes a
weekly number that moves a dated target. What does the research say about
whether people hit a stated savings rate, what makes them abandon it, and
what frequency of review helps rather than demoralises?

**Debt.** Snowball versus avalanche is one of the few genuinely tested
questions here and the answer is more interesting than either camp says.
Cover it, and route to a professional as the constraint requires.

**Financial stress and its health effects.** Real literature, and it
connects this pillar to the rest of the app. Money worry affects sleep;
the app can say so honestly.

**Couples and money.** Joint versus separate accounts has actual research
behind it. Connects to the relationship coach.

**Income variability.** Founders, tradespeople, shift workers with
overtime. Almost all budgeting content assumes a stable fortnightly wage
and this audience often does not have one.

**Insurance and the boring protective layer.** Under-covered everywhere
because nobody sells content about it.

## Contradicted practices to hunt

- The latte factor and small-expense framing as the primary lever
- Budgeting apps and tracking as sufficient on their own
- Most of what is claimed about willpower and spending
- Round-up savings features as materially significant
- The specific-numbers school of emergency-fund advice, which is far more
  confident than its evidence

## Sources worth starting from

**Journals:** Journal of Consumer Research · Journal of Economic
Psychology · Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance · Journal of
Marketing Research · Behavioural Public Policy · Journal of Pension
Economics and Finance

**Researchers:** Annamaria Lusardi (financial literacy) · Shlomo Benartzi
and Richard Thaler (Save More Tomorrow) · Hal Hershfield (future self) ·
Abigail Sussman · Cass Sunstein (defaults)

**Institutional:** ASIC MoneySmart · the ATO · the Productivity
Commission's superannuation work · Behavioural Insights Team publications

**Podcasts, as discovery only:** *Rational Reminder* (academic, Canadian,
unusually free of product interest — the standout in a field that is
mostly selling something). Treat every podcast where the host sells a
product, a course or a platform as an advertisement that happens to
contain some research, and verify accordingly.

## Grade honestly, and expect it to be low

Behavioural finance has a replication problem. Several famous effects in
this literature have failed to replicate or shrunk substantially under
scrutiny. Check before you lean on any classic finding, and grade the
current state of the evidence rather than the state it had in 2010.

A round that returns mostly C and D here would be entirely believable.

## What a good round looks like

15-20 new protocols, taking the pillar from 11 to around 30. Weighted
toward the mechanisms with field-experiment evidence, the Australian
specifics, and income variability. Every constraint respected. And an
honest note on which famous behavioural findings you checked and declined
to use.
