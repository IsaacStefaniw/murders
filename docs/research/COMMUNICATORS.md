# The communicator roster — who to mine, and how to credit them

Companion to `README.md`. Read both before a round.

## Why this file exists

Isaac's ask: *"I want us to have the most robust library of protocols and
findings — that means our coaches embody all of the top performers in this
category."* That is the right ambition and this file is how it gets done
without costing the thing that makes the library worth having.

The library already credits **212 distinct people** across 204 protocols.
The most-credited are Peter Attia (36), Tim Ferriss (34), Andrew Huberman
(32), Rhonda Patrick (16), Jordan Peterson (13). The job now is breadth —
more of the category's genuine top performers, mined properly.

**Two layers, and the order matters.** The communicators are the discovery
and recognition layer: they tell you which practices are worth
investigating and put a name on the card that a person recognises. The
research is the layer on top that decides whether it ships and what grade
it carries. Never the other way round.

## How to mine a transcript

1. Take the practice, never the prose. Nothing is reproduced.
2. Find the study they are pointing at, open it, read the methods.
3. Grade the study. The speaker's confidence is not evidence.
4. Where the episode overstates the paper, record it — usually enthusiasm,
   not dishonesty, and worth knowing either way.
5. Credit them in `attribution` if their public teaching genuinely
   popularised the practice. Not if it did not — a false credit is worse
   than an empty one, and the recognition only works while it is real.

## Tier 1 — cite the literature well, attribute broadly

These people generally point at real papers and describe them roughly
correctly. Start here.

**Peter Attia** (*The Drive*) — longevity, metabolic health, training for
later life, sleep. The most rigorous large-audience voice in the category.
**Rhonda Patrick** (*FoundMyFitness*) — heat and sauna especially, which is
the priority pillar; micronutrients, exercise physiology.
**Andrew Huberman** (*Huberman Lab*) — enormous reach and recognition,
covers most pillars. Use freely *and* verify hard: he has drawn substantive
criticism for overstating findings, and he is already the single
most-credited name in the recovery round. Trace every claim.
**Andy Galpin** (*Perform*) — strength, conditioning, recovery. Excellent.
**Layne Norton** — nutrition and training, unusually good on evidence
quality and study design.
**Greg Nuckols and Eric Trexler** (*Stronger By Science*) — publish their
reasoning; among the best in the field.
**Danny Lennon** (*Sigma Nutrition Radio*) — the most rigorous nutrition
podcast available.
**Gil Carvalho** (*Nutrition Made Simple*) — careful, well-cited.
**Matt Kaeberlein** — longevity, and notably sceptical of longevity hype.
A useful counterweight.
**Ben Felix** (*Rational Reminder*) — money, academically grounded, free of
product interest. Rare in that field.
**Sam Harris** (*Waking Up*, *Making Sense*) — meditation. Rigorous, and a
good route into the contemplative literature including its adverse effects.

## Tier 2 — excellent practice popularisers

Strong on *what to do*, lighter on mechanism. Credit them for the practice;
grade it from the papers.

**Tim Ferriss** — the original tactical-protocol populariser, across every
pillar. Already 34 credits and deservedly.
**Jeff Nippard**, **Mike Israetel**, **Eric Helms**, **Pavel Tsatsouline** —
training.
**Cal Newport** (*Deep Questions*), **Adam Grant** (*WorkLife*) — work.
**Morgan Housel** — money psychology. *The Psychology of Money* is the best
thing in that space for a general audience.
**Scott Pape** (*The Barefoot Investor*) — **Australian, and the single most
relevant money voice for this audience.** Superannuation, offset accounts,
the local system. Add him.
**Ramit Sethi** — money behaviour and money-and-couples, which the
relationship brief also needs.
**Esther Perel** — relationships. Huge recognition.
**Dan Harris** (*10% Happier*) — mind. A good interviewer who surfaces the
researchers worth reading.
**Judson Brewer**, **Kristin Neff**, **Rick Hanson** — mind.
**Barbara Oakley**, **Scott Young** — learning.
**Kirk Parsley**, **Matthew Walker** — sleep. Walker with care: his popular
book has documented factual errors, so verify anything tracing to it
against the primary literature. He is credited 3 times and that is about
right.

## Tier 3 — high recognition, thin evidence

Real name recognition, very little that grades above D on its own. Use them
to find topics. Credit them only where the practice stands on somebody
else's research.

**Tony Robbins** — enormous recognition and almost nothing that meets this
library's evidence bar. Where a practice he popularised has independent
support (implementation intentions, priming a state before a hard task),
credit him beside the researcher. Never alone on a card.
**Joe Rogan** — the largest audience in the category, but an interviewer
rather than a domain expert, and the show carries a very wide range of
claim quality. **Mine the guests, credit the guests.** Rogan's own name on
a protocol card adds little and risks a lot.

## Handle with care — named risks

These are judgement calls where recognition and credibility pull against
each other. Isaac should decide; the round should flag rather than assume.

**David Sinclair.** Credited 3 times now. His central longevity claims —
resveratrol, NMN, NAD precursors — have been substantially challenged, and
he has commercial interests in that space. The library *already excludes
every one of those compounds by policy*. Attributing a longevity card to
him would put a name on the page that the informed half of this audience
associates with exactly the claims we refuse to make. Recommendation: do
not add new Sinclair attributions in longevity or supplements. Elsewhere,
fine.

**Jordan Peterson.** Credited 13 times, mostly on the mind pillar and the
self-authoring and responsibility material, some of which has genuine
research behind it. He is also politically polarising in a way none of the
others are, and a name on a card reaches a person who did not ask for it.
The existing 13 are defensible where the practice stands on its own
evidence. Recommendation: keep, do not expand, and never let his name be
the *only* attribution on a card.

**Anyone selling the thing they are describing.** A supplement line, a
testing service, a course. Not disqualifying — Attia and Ferriss both sell
things — but where the episode is effectively an advertisement, the
framing follows the product. Weight accordingly.

## Where to find transcripts

Most large shows publish them: official sites and show notes (Huberman Lab,
The Drive, Tim Ferriss, Sigma Nutrition all do), YouTube auto-captions for
the rest, and podcast platforms increasingly carry them natively. Show
notes are often better than the transcript for our purposes — the good
shows list the papers directly.

## On paying for journal access

**Not yet.** The recovery round opened ~190 papers without a subscription.
Between PubMed Central, Europe PMC, preprint servers and the fact that much
of sports science and nutrition is open access, access was not the
bottleneck — verification time was.

Cheaper routes before a subscription: an Australian state library card
often carries free database access from home; authors will email a PDF if
asked; ResearchGate frequently has author copies.

Revisit only if a round reports being repeatedly blocked by paywalls, and
say so in `findings.md` naming the journals. Then it is worth buying access
to those specific ones rather than a broad subscription.

## Books, free and legitimate

**NCBI Bookshelf** (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books) is the strongest of these —
full textbooks and clinical references, free, including StatPearls and
Endotext. Genuinely useful for mechanism and background.
**DOAB** and **OAPEN** — open-access academic books.
**Publisher open-access programmes** — Springer, MDPI and others release
full volumes free.
**Author-released chapters** are common; check the author's own site.

Books are background and orientation. They do not set a grade — a
five-year-old book chapter loses to a current meta-analysis every time. And
use only legitimately available copies; shadow libraries are not a source
this project uses.
